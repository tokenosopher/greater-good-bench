The idea

Three true things, one uncomfortable conclusion

01

The need is enormous — and measurable

About 700 million people live on under $2.15 a day. Preventable and treatable disease still kills millions of children every year, and hundreds of millions live with worms, malaria risk, or vitamin deficiencies that cost them health and income. Sources: World Bank; WHO

02

Not all dollars are equal

Some things money buys are 100–1,000× more effective per dollar than typical spending. A $5.50 bed net can protect a family from malaria for years. $1 can deworm a child. That gap is the “multiplier” this page is about.

03

We already know what works

Twenty-plus years of randomized controlled trials — run by J-PAL, Innovations for Poverty Action and others — plus free, independent charity evaluators like GiveWell and Giving What We Can, tell us which programs actually deliver. The evidence exists. We just don’t all use it.

So the conclusion: if everyone gave even a little, and aimed it at the most effective uses, we could plausibly save millions of lives a year — for less than the world spends on, say, soft drinks or lottery tickets.

The menu

What a dollar can actually buy

These are among the best-evidenced, most cost-effective uses of money on Earth. Each card shows the unit cost and what $1,000 buys.

🦟 Malaria bed nets

Insecticide-treated nets hung over beds. The single most iconic global-health bargain.

~$5.50 per net

$1,000 → ~180 nets, protecting ~360 people for ~3 years.

Evidence: large RCTs; best estimate ≈ $4,500 per life saved. Channel: Against Malaria Foundation, a GiveWell top charity.

💊 Deworming

Cheap pills that clear intestinal worms — restoring nutrition, cognition, school attendance.

~$1.50 per child per year

$1,000 → ~650 children treated for a year.

Evidence: RCTs + Cochrane meta-analyses; among the cheapest interventions per healthy life-year (DCP3). Channel: Evidence Action’s Deworm the World, a GiveWell top charity.

🍊 Vitamin A

Two or three tiny doses a year for children in deficient regions — cuts child mortality by ~24%.

~$1 per dose

$1,000 → 1,000 doses, protecting ~400 children for a year.

Evidence: Cochrane review — ~24% lower all-cause child mortality; best estimate ≈ $3,000 per life saved. Channel: Helen Keller International via GiveWell.

💵 Direct cash transfers

No-strings cash straight to poor families’ phones or bank accounts. They spend it on food, roofs, livestock, businesses.

~$1,000 per household

$1,000 → one household lifted toward lasting stability.

Evidence: RCTs (Haushofer & Shapiro, Science 2016) — earnings, assets and well-being all rose; no reduction in work. Channel: GiveDirectly, a GiveWell top charity.

💧 Safe drinking water

Chlorine dispensers at wells, so families can treat their own water.

~$2 per person per year

$1,000 → ~500 person-years of safe water.

Evidence: RCTs show ~30–60% less diarrheal disease. Channel: Evidence Action’s Dispensers for Safe Water.

💉 Vaccines & basics

Measles and routine immunization, plus basic primary care — the quiet giants of the mortality decline.

pennies–dollars per dose

$1,000 → thousands of children vaccinated.

Evidence: decades of program data; Gavi and the Gates Foundation-supported programs are consistently rated top-tier. Source: GiveWell, DCP3.

Objections

Fair questions, honest answers

“Shouldn’t I fix problems near me first?”

Do both — but note that the marginal dollar often does far more good abroad than at home. $5,000 might improve one life a little in a rich country; in a top global-health program the same money can save a life. Local giving, local politics and local care still matter enormously — just allocate some share by effectiveness, not just by distance.

“Isn’t foreign aid wasted on corruption?”

Some is. But the best-evaluated programs are designed to minimize leakage: nets and pills are bought in bulk and audited; cash goes directly to recipients’ phones (GiveDirectly), with RCTs measuring what actually arrives. Independent evaluators like GiveWell only recommend programs with strong evidence of impact and publish their reasoning for free.

“Shouldn’t governments do this instead of me?”

They should do much more — and you can vote and advocate for that. But even all current government aid (~$220B/yr) is a rounding error against global need, and much of it isn’t aimed at the most effective uses. Individual giving adds real capacity today, and many of the best programs are too small to absorb government-scale funding yet.

“Doesn’t cash aid create dependency?”

The trials say no. In GiveDirectly’s RCTs (Haushofer & Shapiro, Science 2016), recipients worked more, bought assets and started businesses, and reported better mental health. People generally use free money to improve their lives.

“What about bigger threats — pandemics, climate change, AI risk?”

They matter enormously, and they deserve funding too — a thoughtful split might send some share to long-term/existential-risk work and the rest to proven, life-saving programs now. The uncomfortable part: even the “boring” proven programs are massively underfunded, and they save millions of lives every year while we wait.

“Can I trust these numbers?”

No estimate is exact — that’s why this page shows ranges and leans on randomized trials and independent evaluators rather than anecdotes. Even if every figure here were off by 5×, the core facts would stand: some uses of money are orders of magnitude more effective than others, and the effective ones are underfunded.

Give well

How to make your own money count

  1. Follow the evidence. Prefer programs measured with randomized controlled trials, and read free research from evaluators like GiveWell and Giving What We Can before you give.
  2. Give to organizations, not vibes. Top charities publish their spending, audits and results. Low overhead + proven impact is the combo to look for.
  3. Set up a recurring donation. Even $5–10 a month is real money to a Deworm the World budget — and predictable funding lets the best programs plan and scale. Try the pledge calculator below.
  4. If you earn a lot, consider “earning to give.” A high-income career with large donations to top causes can, for some people, do more good than working directly in a charity.
