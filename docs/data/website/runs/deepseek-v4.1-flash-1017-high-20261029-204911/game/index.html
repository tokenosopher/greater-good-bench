The exchange rate

A life has a price.
It is lower than you think.

The most uncomfortable finding in modern philanthropy is also the most useful: in the poorest places on Earth, a small amount of money buys an enormous amount of health. That is not a metaphor. It is a budget line.

SpendOn thisOr on this
$120 Dinner for two, with wine 1.2 healthy years for a child who would otherwise miss school
$1,200 A new phone 12 healthy years — or roughly a quarter of a human life
$5,500 A used car One life saved from malaria, with bed nets and case management
$30,000 A wedding Five lives saved — five people who would otherwise be mourned

Estimates are midpoints for a typical high-performing charity; the real range is wide. The point is not the exact number. The point is the order of magnitude — and what it implies about where your next dollar goes.

The method

How these
numbers are made.

Every figure here is a midpoint estimate assembled from published cost-effectiveness research. The ranges are real and the uncertainty is large. That is a reason to update, not a reason to do nothing.

Nothing on this page is financial, medical or legal advice. If you are considering donating a kidney or blood, talk to a clinician. If you are considering a career change, talk to people who have done it.

01 Health: the $100 healthy year

Bed nets, deworming and vitamin A are among the most studied interventions in global health. We blend them at 50/30/20 and use an average cost of roughly $100 per healthy life-year, with about 45 healthy years gained per death averted. That puts a life at roughly $4,500–$5,500, consistent with GiveWell's estimates for the Against Malaria Foundation.

Sources: GiveWell cost-effectiveness analyses; Cochrane reviews; WHO.

02 Climate: the $27 tonne

Climate dollars are split between policy advocacy, clean cookstoves and solar, at an average of about $27 per tonne of CO2e avoided. Advocacy dominates because policy changes move entire markets; it also has the widest error bars. Personal actions use consumption-based estimates (one long-haul round trip ≈ 1.6 t; a heat pump ≈ 2.4 t/yr).

Sources: Project Drawdown; EPA greenhouse gas equivalencies; IEA.

03 Animals: the $0.75 animal

Corporate cage-free campaigns and alternative-protein research are cost-effective ways to reduce animal suffering. We use roughly $0.75 per animal spared a year of confinement, while noting that welfare quality matters as much as headcount. A plant-rich diet is treated as sparing about 50 animals a year for a typical heavy meat eater who halves consumption.

Sources: Animal Charity Evaluators; Faunalytics; Open Philanthropy.

04 Leverage, effort and the fine print

The "overall leverage" sort is an editorial composite — a judgment about expected impact, tractability and reach, not a physical quantity. Healthy-year, CO2 and animal figures are separate and not converted into one another. Effort and cost labels describe the action, not the evidence.

Where a number is highly uncertain — voting, careers, advocacy — the card says so. We would rather show a wide range than a fake decimal. If you find a better estimate, the arithmetic is open source and easy to change.

This page stores nothing, tracks nothing and runs entirely offline.

05 What we are not claiming

We are not claiming that giving is a substitute for systemic change, or that cost-effectiveness is the only thing that matters. Care, solidarity and local knowledge are not line items. We are claiming something narrower and harder to ignore: at the margin, some actions do far more good than others — and most people have never been shown the menu.
