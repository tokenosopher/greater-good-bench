The objections — taken seriously

You're right to be skeptical. Here are the honest answers.

“I'm not rich. I can barely pay my own bills.”

“Rich” is relative — and you're reading this on a computer. If you earn $40,000 a year, you out-earn roughly 94% of humanity. You don't need to be a billionaire to save a life; you need $5,000 — often less — and a well-chosen charity. For most people in the global top 10%, that's months, not decades, of discretionary spending.

“My donation is a drop in the ocean.”

A life is not an ocean. $5,000 to a top-rated malaria charity is, with high probability, a specific child who doesn't die. Almost no one ever saves a life. You can do it within a year — quietly, anonymously, from a couch.

“Charities are wasteful and corrupt.”

This was the hard problem, and it's been solved about as well as it can be. GiveWell spends years auditing charities and publishes all of its math, mistakes included. Its top charities deliver bed nets for ~$5 and vitamin A for ~$1.25, with independent monitoring and randomized-trial evidence. If you distrust one charity, that's an argument for better giving, not for no giving — and “no giving” has a body count too.

“Aid creates dependency.”

Bed nets, deworming and vitamin A are cheap, time-limited, and measured by randomized controlled trials; programs are increasingly handed to national governments to run themselves. Since 2000, simple interventions like these have helped cut child deaths from ~10 million a year to under 5 million — one of the best-evidenced success stories in all of development.

“We should fix the root causes instead.”

Do both — the critique is fair, and it applies to your consumption too. But a child with malaria tonight cannot wait for structural reform, and bed nets work tonight. Giving and system-change aren't rivals; the same dollar just can't do both, so the decision deserves to be deliberate rather than default.

“I already give to local causes / my alma mater.”

Local causes matter, and that's your call. But the math is stubborn: $5,000 in a rich country might buy a nicer mural or a marginally better library; $5,000 in the poorest places buys a life. The whole point of this page is one question: if you're giving anyway, how much good do you want it to do?
