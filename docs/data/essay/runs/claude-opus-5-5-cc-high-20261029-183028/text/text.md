# To Everyone, at Once

*An essay written for every person alive*

---

This essay was written for everyone. Not for everyone who agrees with me, or who speaks a certain language, or who reads a certain kind of book. It's for every person alive.

So as you read it, picture who else is reading it. Your mother. Your neighbour. The person you like least. A woman selling vegetables at a market, between customers. A soldier on the far side of a border, maybe your border. A twelve-year-old girl. A president. A man in prison with a lot of time to read. Someone who is very rich, and someone who has not eaten today.

That changes what an essay can do.

An essay read by one person can change one mind, if it's lucky. An essay read by everyone can do something stranger. It can create things that we all know, *and that we all know everyone else knows*. Philosophers call this **common knowledge**, and a great deal of human life runs on it.

Think about a traffic light. When it turns green, it isn't enough that you see green. You drive through because you know the driver on the other road sees red, and you know that they know you see green. Take away that shared knowledge and the light is just a coloured lamp. Nobody would trust it.

A lot of the suffering in the world doesn't exist because people are wicked. It exists because people don't know something that someone else knows. Or because each person is waiting for someone else to move first. Or because we believe things about each other that aren't true. An essay can reach problems like these.

So I'll try to do three things. First, I'll share some facts that save lives. Second, I'll correct some beliefs we hold about each other that are false and dangerous. Third, I'll say a few things about how to live that I think are true wherever you are.

I'll try to say only things that are true, that apply wherever you live, and that you can act on. Where I rely on research, the sources are listed at the end. Where I'm only giving my own judgment, I'll say so.

You'll already know some of this. Please read it anyway. The point is no longer just that *you* know it. Now everyone knows it, and everyone knows that everyone knows.

---

## 1. Most of the world's suffering is not a mystery

Start with something that ought to be famous and isn't.

In the year 1800, roughly four out of every ten children born anywhere on Earth died before their fifth birthday. That was true in palaces and in huts, in every country and every religion. For all of human history before that, it was probably about the same. Almost every parent buried a child. Almost every child lost a brother or a sister.

Today, around four in a hundred children die before age five. That's still far too many: nearly five million small children a year. But it's a tenth of what it was. It's one of the greatest things human beings have ever done, and most people alive have never been told.

No miracle did it. Mostly it was ordinary things: clean water, soap, vaccines, nets over beds, better food. It was mothers who had stayed in school and knew more. And it was a mixture of salt and sugar.

In 1971, war in what's now Bangladesh drove millions of refugees into camps across the border in India. Cholera broke out. Cholera kills by draining water from the body, and the usual treatment was fluid given through a needle into a vein. There weren't nearly enough needles, fluid or trained hands. A young doctor named Dilip Mahalanabis made a different choice. His team gave people a solution of salt and sugar in water *to drink*, and they taught families to give it themselves. In the camps where it was used, the death rate from cholera fell from around 30 percent to under 4 percent. Later the medical journal *The Lancet* called the treatment "potentially the most important medical advance this century." It's thought to have saved tens of millions of lives since then. It costs almost nothing.

I take two lessons from this.

**The first is that fatalism is false.** The things that killed half of all children for all of human history turned out to be fixable. Ordinary people fixed them, many of whose names you will never hear. So when someone tells you that some suffering is "just how the world is," be suspicious. It might be. It's often not.

**The second is that much of what's left is a problem of reach, not of knowledge.** Most of the children who will die this year will die of things the world already knows how to prevent. The gap between what humanity knows and what each family knows is one of the deadliest gaps on Earth. An essay that everyone reads can close a little of it. That's where we'll start.

## 2. Things everyone should know, because they save lives

If you're a doctor or a nurse, all of this will seem obvious to you. Read it anyway, so you know your patients have read it too. Most of the people who die of these causes don't die because the knowledge doesn't exist. They die because it didn't reach them in time.

### When a child has diarrhoea

Diarrhoea still kills hundreds of thousands of small children every year. The thing that kills them is losing water and salt from the body, not the illness itself, and that can be replaced.

- **If you have oral rehydration salts (ORS) packets, use them.** They are cheap and they're the best option. Mix them exactly as the packet says.
- **If you don't have packets, make the solution at home.** Take **one litre of clean water** (boiled and cooled, if you can). Stir in **six level teaspoons of sugar** and **half a level teaspoon of salt** until they dissolve. It should taste no saltier than tears. Too much salt is dangerous, so measure carefully.
- Give it in **small sips, often**. Use a spoon or cup for a small child. If the child vomits, wait ten minutes and then start again, more slowly.
- **Keep feeding the child. Keep breastfeeding.** Don't hold back food.
- If a health worker can give you **zinc** tablets, give them for 10 to 14 days. They shorten the illness and help prevent the next one.
- **Get help immediately** if the child can't drink, is unusually sleepy or hard to wake, has blood in the stool, or vomits everything.

And wash your hands **with soap**: after using the toilet, after cleaning a child, and before preparing or eating food. Water alone isn't enough. In studies, handwashing with soap cuts diarrhoea by roughly a third.

### Small children and water

Around 300,000 people drown every year, and children aged one to four are among those most at risk. Most people imagine drowning as splashing and shouting. **Real drowning is usually silent.** A small child slips under the water without a sound. It can take less than a minute, and it can happen in very shallow water: a bucket, a bathtub, a ditch, a well.

- **When small children are near water, an adult must be watching them.** Actually watching. Not glancing up now and then from a phone or a task.
- **Cover wells, water tanks and buckets. Fence ponds and pools.** Empty containers after use.
- **Teach children who are old enough to learn how to swim**, and teach them about water safety.
- Where many parents have to work, a shared place where someone watches the small children saves lives. In parts of Bangladesh, village day-care for young children sharply reduced drownings.

### On the road

About 1.2 million people die on the roads every year. For people aged five to twenty-nine, road crashes are the leading cause of death in the world.

- **Wear a seatbelt, in every seat, on every trip, however short.** It cuts the risk of death for people in the front seats by about half.
- **On a motorbike or scooter, wear a helmet, and fasten the strap.** A helmet worn properly cuts the risk of death by about 40 percent and the risk of serious head injury by about 70 percent.
- **Put children in a child seat or a proper booster, in the back.**
- **Slow down.** Speed matters more than it feels like it does, because the force of a crash grows much faster than the speed. An adult pedestrian hit by a car at under 50 km/h will usually survive. At 80 km/h, they will usually die.
- **Never drive after drinking, when you're very tired, or while using your phone.**

### Tobacco

Tobacco kills more than 8 million people a year. About 1.3 million of them never used it and only breathed other people's smoke. **It kills up to half of the people who keep using it.**

If you smoke or chew tobacco, you already know this, and I'm not going to lecture you. Here are two things you might not know. **Quitting helps at any age.** People who stop before about age 40 avoid around 90 percent of the extra risk of dying early. And **most people who quit for good failed several times first.** Failing doesn't prove you can't stop. It's usually part of how stopping happens. If help is available where you live, use it. And keep smoke away from children.

### Smoke inside the home

Cooking and heating with open fires and smoky fuels inside the home is linked to more than three million deaths a year. Most of the people harmed are women and small children, because they spend the most time near the fire.

- Where you can, **cook with a door and window open**, or outside, or under a chimney or hood.
- **Keep babies and small children away from smoke.**
- When you can afford it, use cleaner fuels and stoves.
- **Never burn charcoal or run a generator inside a closed room.** It gives off carbon monoxide, a gas you can't see or smell, and it kills people while they sleep.

### Vaccines

Over the last fifty years, vaccines given to children around the world have saved an estimated 154 million lives. That's about six lives every minute, for half a century. The measles vaccine alone accounts for the largest share. Before it existed, measles killed more than two million people a year, most of them children.

Like every medicine, vaccines can have side effects, and serious ones are rare. The diseases they prevent are far more dangerous. Few conclusions in medicine have been tested as thoroughly as this one. **Vaccinate your children on schedule**, and finish the full course.

### Newborn babies

Nearly half of all deaths of children under five happen in the first month of life. Some simple things help:

- **Put the baby on the mother's bare chest straight after birth**, skin against skin, and keep the baby warm. In a large World Health Organization trial, starting this "kangaroo care" right away for small, early babies cut deaths by about a quarter.
- **Start breastfeeding within the first hour**, and if you can, give only breast milk for the first six months.
- **Get help immediately** if a newborn won't feed, has trouble breathing, has a fever or is unusually cold, or is very still.

### When someone might be thinking about suicide

More than 700,000 people end their own lives every year, and many more try to. A common fear stops people from helping: *if I ask, I might put the idea into their head.*

**You won't.** Research has looked at this again and again. Asking someone directly about suicide does not make it more likely, and it often brings relief.

If you're worried about someone:

- **Ask plainly:** "Are you thinking about killing yourself?" Use those words or ones just as clear.
- **Listen.** Don't argue, lecture, or rush to fix it. Stay with them.
- **Put distance between them and the means**: pesticides, guns, stockpiled pills. Suicidal crises often pass, and what a person can reach during the worst hours matters enormously. After Sri Lanka banned the most poisonous pesticides, its suicide rate fell sharply, and tens of thousands of people lived who would otherwise have died.
- **Help them reach help**: a doctor, a health worker, a crisis line, someone they trust. Don't leave them alone if the danger is close.

---

*None of these is complicated. That's the point. If each of us passes even one of these facts to one person who can't read, or who has forgotten, or who was never told, the number of lives saved will be very large. Say them out loud. Teach them to your children. Write the salt-and-sugar recipe somewhere in your home.*

## 3. We agree more than we think

Now for a different kind of mistake: one about other people's minds.

In the early 1990s, psychologists at an American university asked students how comfortable they were with the heavy drinking on campus. Then they asked how comfortable they thought the *typical* student was. Most students were privately uneasy about it. But each one assumed everyone else was fine with it, because nobody said otherwise. So everyone kept going along with a norm that most of them didn't like.

That's a small example of something with a name: **pluralistic ignorance**. Each person hides their private view behind everyone else's public silence, and then reads that silence as agreement.

Larger examples:

- **In Saudi Arabia**, researchers found that most young married men privately supported women working outside the home. But they greatly *underestimated* how many other men agreed with them. When some of the men were shown the true numbers, they became more likely to help their wives look for work.
- **Around the world**, a 2024 survey of about 130,000 people in 125 countries found that 69 percent said they'd be willing to give 1 percent of their income every month to fight climate change. Almost nine in ten wanted their governments to do more. But when asked how many of their *fellow citizens* would be willing, people guessed fewer than half. A large majority believed they were a minority.

Think about what that means. The obstacle to change isn't always that people disagree. Sometimes everyone agrees in private and nobody knows it.

Here's what can happen when people find out. For about a thousand years, many families in China bound the feet of their young daughters. The bones were broken and folded so that girls grew up crippled and in pain. Parents did it partly because their daughters would otherwise be unable to marry. No single family could stop alone. Then in the late 1800s and early 1900s, reformers began organising associations whose members pledged in public that they wouldn't bind their daughters' feet, *and* that they would let their sons marry women with unbound feet. Once enough families knew that enough other families had pledged, the trap opened. In many regions, a practice a thousand years old disappeared within a generation. More recently, communities in Senegal and elsewhere in West Africa used the same idea, public declarations made together, to abandon female genital cutting.

Nobody won those changes by converting people one at a time. They won them by **making private agreement public**, so that everyone could see they weren't alone.

**So here is the first thing this essay asks you to do with the fact that everyone has read it.** Sometime soon, you'll hold a private view that is kind, or fair, or sensible, and you'll stay quiet because you assume you're the only one. You probably aren't. Say it out loud, gently, in a way that makes it easier for the next person to say it too. Someone near you has been waiting for exactly that.

This works in the other direction too, and it matters just as much. **The loudest voices are usually not the majority.** One study of false news during a United States election found that just 0.1 percent of users shared about 80 percent of it. Studies across several countries have found that most of the hostility in online political arguments comes from a fairly small number of people, and the same people tend to be hostile offline as well. The internet isn't a mirror held up to humanity. It's more like a fairground mirror, and it makes the angriest few look enormous.

None of this means the majority is always right. Majorities have been cruel, and lone voices have been right. My point is narrower: **we should see each other accurately.** Right now, most of us don't.

## 4. The people you hate are mostly not who you think

This is the error I believe does the most damage, so I'll stay on it a little longer.

Researchers have asked people on opposing sides of political divides two questions. How much do you dislike the other side? And how much do you think *they* dislike *you*? Again and again, both sides greatly overestimate how much the other side hates them. In the United States, people on each side believed the other side dehumanised them about twice as much as it really did. A study of 26 countries found the same pattern: people's beliefs about what the other side thinks of them were consistently more negative than the truth.

This matters because people respond to the enemy they imagine. If you believe the other side wants to destroy you, defending yourself by any means starts to seem reasonable. In one experiment, people were simply shown accurate figures on how little the other side supported political violence. Their *own* support for violence fell.

Why do we get each other so wrong? Partly because of the fairground mirror I just described. And partly because, in many places, **your anger has become a product that is bought and sold.** One study found that each extra moral or emotional word in a political message on social media raised its spread by about 20 percent. Another found that posts attacking the opposing group were shared far more than posts praising one's own. Some companies, broadcasters and politicians earn more when you're furious. That doesn't make your anger wrong. It does mean you should check it before you let it make decisions for you.

There's a darker reason this matters. Before the genocide in Rwanda in 1994, a popular radio station spent months calling Tutsi people "cockroaches." Nazi propaganda called Jews rats and a disease. Again and again in history, mass killing has started with words that turn neighbours into animals, insects, filth or infection. **When you hear any group of human beings described as vermin, a plague, a flood or an infestation, recognise that sound.** It's the sound of something very dangerous beginning. It doesn't matter who is saying it or who it's said about.

Here's a thought I find hard to escape. If you'd been born where they were born, to their parents, in their year, and lived through what they lived through, you would very probably believe what they believe. Nobody chooses the country, the religion, the family or the news they grow up inside. Most of what any of us believes, we were handed.

That isn't an argument that nothing is wrong. Some acts are wrong. You may oppose them with everything you have. But you can fight what a person *does* without pretending they aren't a person. The people who have ended the bitterest conflicts often understood this best. Nelson Mandela spent 27 years in prison, and while he was there he learned the language of his jailers and studied their history. He wanted to understand them. That understanding was part of how a country that many expected to collapse into civil war found its way into a peaceful transition instead.

So, a small habit. **Before you decide that a whole group of people is evil or stupid, try to describe their view in words they'd agree with.** If you can't, you don't understand it yet. And if you can, you'll probably find you hate it less, even if you still disagree.

## 5. How to be wrong

Every person reading this believes some things that are false. So do I. This essay almost certainly contains mistakes, though I've tried hard to avoid them. Being wrong is not the danger. Being *unable to notice* when you're wrong is the danger.

Some habits that help, from my own judgment and from what research on reasoning suggests:

- **Ask yourself: what would change my mind?** If the honest answer is "nothing," then what you have isn't a belief any more. It has become part of who you are, and nothing can test it.
- **Be extra suspicious of beliefs that flatter you or your group.** We find it easy to believe whatever makes us look good and our opponents look bad.
- **Notice when certainty and anger arrive together.** That pairing is very common in people who are being manipulated.
- **Remember that hearing something often doesn't make it true.** Repetition makes a claim *feel* true, and psychologists have shown this many times. Familiar isn't the same as correct.
- **Before you share something that makes you furious, wait.** Ask where it came from. Ask who gains if you believe it.
- **Let other people change their minds without humiliating them.** If every changed mind is treated as a defeat, nobody will ever change their mind in public, and then nobody will learn anything.

That last one is something only an essay read by everyone can propose. **So let's agree on it, all of us, now: changing your mind in public, because of good reasons, is honourable.** It isn't weakness. It's rare, it takes courage, and it's how every human being improves. When you see someone do it, respect them for it.

## 6. Children

Every adult who has ever done great harm or great good was once a child. Much of what happens in the world starts in what happens to children.

**Hitting children doesn't make them better.** Across the world, hundreds of millions of small children are regularly hit or shouted at as discipline. Many parents believe it's necessary. Researchers have studied this closely, with data from more than 160,000 children. Physical punishment is linked to *more* aggression, more anxiety and depression, more behaviour problems, and worse relationships between parents and children. It doesn't improve behaviour over the long run.

Most parents who hit their children love them, and most were hit themselves. This isn't an accusation. It's information our grandparents didn't have. What tends to work better: stay calm, or step away until you are. Set a few clear, simple rules and keep them consistent. Explain the reasons. Notice and praise good behaviour more than you punish bad behaviour. Use consequences that make sense and are fair. And give warmth, constantly. It's not weakness. It's where good behaviour grows from.

**Talk to babies.** A baby's brain grows through back-and-forth with the people around them. Talk to them, sing, answer their sounds and faces as if it's a conversation, and read to them if you can. It costs nothing and it shapes the rest of their life.

**Keep children safe from abuse.** Most children who are sexually abused are harmed by someone they know. Teach children the correct names for their body parts. Tell them that their body belongs to them, that no one should touch them in private places, and that there must never be secrets from you about touching. Above all, tell them that **they will never be in trouble for telling you**, and if they do tell you, believe them.

**Let girls finish school.** Every year, about twelve million girls are married before the age of eighteen. Girls who stay in school longer tend to marry later and have healthier children, and they and their families tend to be better off. If you are ever part of deciding whether a girl stays in school or marries young, please choose school.

## 7. If you have more than you need

Many people reading this are struggling. If that's you, you can skip this section. Or read it, so you know what the others are being asked to do.

But if you finish most months with something left over, you are richer than most people who have ever lived. If you earn a typical salary in a wealthy country, you are very likely richer than nine out of ten people alive today. Unless you've travelled widely, that can be hard to feel. The fact still matters, because **a small amount of money, moved from where it's plentiful to where it's scarce, does an astonishing amount of good.**

Charity evaluators have studied which programmes save the most lives for each dollar. Their estimates suggest that the best ones save a child's life for a few thousand US dollars. These include bed nets against malaria, seasonal medicine that protects small children from malaria, vitamin A, and support for families to get their children vaccinated. Very few things you could do with that amount of money matter more.

Here's my suggestion, and it's only a suggestion. **Choose a share of your income, decide it once and in advance, and give it every year to whatever does the most good you can find.** For some people that's 1 percent. Some choose 10 percent. Deciding in advance matters, because it spares you from wrestling with the decision every month. Don't give *instead* of helping the people near you. Give *as well*.

If you have time but not money, here is what you can give that money can't buy: **real attention, for one person who has no one.** In many places loneliness is now common and quietly serious. One visit, one phone call or one meal with someone can be enormous to them.

## 8. If you are in pain right now

Out of everyone reading this, hundreds of millions are suffering right now. Some are depressed. Some are grieving. Some are living through war, or hunger, or violence in their own home. If that's you, I can't fix it with words, and I won't pretend to. But here are some things that are true:

- **What you feel now isn't a permanent forecast.** Even the worst feelings change, though they lie and tell you they never will.
- **Tell one person.** Pain that's shared, even with one person, changes shape. Asking for help isn't weakness. It's what people do.
- **If someone is hurting you, it's not your fault.** You're allowed to seek help, and you're allowed to leave.
- **If you are thinking of ending your life, please tell someone today.** Call your local emergency number or a crisis line, or talk to a health worker or someone you trust. If you can, put distance between yourself and whatever you might use. The worst hours do pass.

And here is something only this essay can say to you. **The person you tell has read this too.** They read the part about asking plainly, listening and staying. They know what to do. You won't have to explain everything.

## 9. What lasts

Since 1938, researchers at Harvard University have followed the lives of hundreds of men, and later their wives and children, from youth into old age. It's one of the longest studies of human lives ever carried out. Its most striking finding is easy to say. **The quality of people's close relationships predicted their health and happiness better than almost anything else.** The people most satisfied in their relationships at age 50 were the healthiest at age 80. Their relationships predicted their health better than their cholesterol levels did.

Other research shows that we underestimate how much small acts of connection mean to people. When people write a letter of thanks, they expect the person reading it to feel a little pleased, and maybe a bit awkward. In fact the readers are usually deeply moved. People expect that talking to a stranger on a train will be unpleasant. In fact they usually come away happier. We hold back from kindness because we misjudge how it will land.

So:

- **Tell the people you love that you love them, and say why.** Do it while they're alive to hear it.
- **Thank someone who changed your life**, specifically, in words or in writing.
- **If it's safe, repair one broken relationship.** Or at least put down the grudge, even if you can't put down the memory.
- **Talk to someone you'd normally walk past.**

Everyone who reads this will die. It's one of the few things all eight billion of us share for certain. I've never heard of anyone at the end of their life wishing they had been angrier, or more certain, or that they had spent more time proving strangers wrong.

## 10. A word to those with power

If you lead a country, an army, a company, a religious community, a school or a family, then everyone you lead has read this too. And they know that you have.

Power makes it easy to forget that the people below you are as real as you are. It surrounds you with people who tell you what you'd like to hear. So **seek out the people who'll tell you that you're wrong, and protect them.** Powerful people have made their worst mistakes in rooms where nobody dared to disagree.

Wars are decided by people who will mostly not die in them. If you ever hold that decision, think of the soldiers, and the families on both sides, as clearly as you think of yourself.

And remember who history honours most. Many of the leaders who are most admired were the ones who **gave power back when they could have kept it.** George Washington stepped down after two terms. Nelson Mandela served a single term as president and then left. Judge yourself by how the weakest people in your care are doing, not by how the strongest feel about you.

## 11. Tomorrow

An essay can't change the world. It's only words. The world changes through billions of small decisions, made by ordinary people on ordinary days. So here is what this essay asks of you, tomorrow and after:

1. **Learn the salt-and-sugar recipe** — one litre of clean water, six level teaspoons of sugar, half a level teaspoon of salt — **and teach it to someone.**
2. **Wear your seatbelt or helmet.** Never leave a small child alone near water.
3. **If you use tobacco, try to stop.** If you've failed before, try again. That's how it usually works.
4. **Vaccinate your children.**
5. **Ask someone how they really are.** If you're worried, ask the direct question, and listen.
6. **Say one true and decent thing you've kept quiet** because you thought you were alone.
7. **Before you hate a group of people, describe their view in words they'd accept.**
8. **Change your mind about something, out loud**, and respect others who do.
9. **Don't hit a child.** Talk to a baby.
10. **If you have more than you need, decide what share you'll give away.**
11. **Tell someone you love them.**

That's all. None of it is heroic. All of it, done by enough people, would change the world more than any single hero could.

Tomorrow, you'll meet someone: a stranger, a colleague, a person on the other side of an argument, maybe someone you've feared. They've read this too. They know what you know, and they know you know it.

Start there.
