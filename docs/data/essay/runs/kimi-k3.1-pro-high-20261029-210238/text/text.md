# The Next Person

A better world is not waiting for a better species.

It is waiting, in part, for people like us—distracted, frightened, generous, inconsistent people—to make one small change in how we move through a day: **treat the humanity of others as real, especially when it would be easier not to.**

This sounds too modest to matter. We are surrounded by emergencies so large that private decency can feel like bringing a cup of water to a wildfire. Wars, poverty, climate disruption, loneliness, and abuse will not be solved by remembering that a cashier has an inner life.

But the opposite lesson—that small conduct does not matter—is false. Large harms require institutions, technologies, laws, and organized effort. They also pass, again and again, through ordinary moments: a supervisor deciding whether humiliation is an acceptable management tool; a nurse deciding whether a confused patient is worth another minute; a voter deciding whether cruelty is entertaining; a neighbor deciding whether to knock; an engineer deciding whether an obvious risk can be ignored; a bystander deciding that someone else's suffering is none of their business.

The world is made of systems, but systems are operated in moments. And in nearly every moment, there is a next person.

## The reality we keep editing out

Every stranger contains a life as detailed as yours. Their mouth has tasted particular foods. Some ordinary song can return them to a lost summer. They have a private way of naming their fears. There is a version of tonight in which they sleep peacefully, and a version in which something you do helps keep them awake.

We know this, yet we cannot continuously feel it. The mind simplifies. A driver becomes an obstacle. A caller becomes a task. A crowd becomes an opinion. A distant victim becomes a number. This simplification is not always malicious; it is how limited attention copes with an unlimited world. But if we never interrupt it, convenience quietly becomes our morality. People matter when they are useful, familiar, attractive, or near. They recede when helping them costs something.

The interruption can be almost absurdly small: **What is this like for them?**

Not “What do I think of them?” Not “What label explains them?” Not “Whose side are they on?” Those questions may have their uses. The first question is different. It asks you to restore the interior that your haste removed.

This does not require approving of what someone does. A person can have reasons without having good reasons. You can understand fear without surrendering to it, explain cruelty without excusing it, and protect yourself without needing to make another person worthless. Seeing humanity is not the abandonment of judgment. It is the refusal to let judgment erase reality.

## Use power where you actually have it

We often discuss goodness as though its main arena were the heroic exception: the rescue, the great sacrifice, the life changed in a single act. Most of our influence is less dramatic and more repetitive. It lies in what people can predict from us.

Can the people around you tell you the truth without being punished for it?

Can someone make an honest mistake in your presence and retain their dignity?

Can a person with less status disagree with you?

Can your child fail without wondering whether your love has been withdrawn?

Can your colleague ask a basic question without being made to feel small?

Can a stranger be wrong in your vicinity without becoming material for your contempt?

These are questions about power. Power is not only held by presidents and billionaires. It exists wherever one person's choices substantially affect another's options. The parent has it. So do the popular student, the experienced worker, the physically strong, the person holding the pen, the person who knows the rules, the person everyone else in the room will believe.

A useful rule follows:

> When you have more power, make honesty cheaper. When you have less power, preserve what honesty you can.

Much avoidable suffering is caused by making truth expensive. Patients conceal symptoms because doctors are impatient. Employees conceal defects because leaders reward only good news. Children conceal confusion because adults mock it. Citizens conceal doubts because their group demands certainty. Then decisions are made in a fog assembled from everyone's fear.

If you want better outcomes, do not merely demand courage from people below you. Lower the price of candor. Ask, “What am I missing?” and then respond to the answer in a way that makes the next person willing to answer too. Separate the bearer of bad news from the news. Admit your own errors before requiring confession from others. Praise the person who catches the flaw, including when the flaw is yours.

A household, workplace, or nation in which reality can safely be spoken is not guaranteed to be wise. But one in which reality must be disguised is almost guaranteed to make preventable mistakes.

## Do not make people earn basic decency

There is a kind of moral accounting that consumes enormous energy: deciding who deserves patience. We inspect motives, compare wounds, tally past offenses, and wait for others to become worthy of treatment we would like to receive.

Some distinctions matter. Trust must be earned. Access may need to be restricted. Forgiveness cannot be demanded, and reconciliation without safety can be another injury. Decency is different. It is not a medal for the virtuous. It is a floor below which we refuse to let conduct fall—including our own.

You can fire someone without ridiculing them. You can end a relationship without recruiting an audience. You can punish a crime without celebrating torment. You can disagree without pretending that an opponent's suffering would be delightful. You can enforce a boundary clearly enough that cruelty becomes unnecessary.

This floor protects others, but it also protects you from the slow corruption of practicing contempt. Contempt feels efficient. It converts complexity into a satisfying verdict: they are stupid, rotten, less than us. Its pleasure is the pleasure of no longer having to consider. Yet a mind trained to enjoy degradation will not remain carefully selective. It will find more targets. Eventually, it may turn on you.

The alternative is not universal approval. It is accuracy plus restraint: describe what happened, name its consequences, do what protection and justice require, and add no suffering merely for the pleasure of adding it.

## Let repair be real

If decency were possible only for people who had never caused harm, it would be available to no one.

We will interrupt, assume, exclude, snap, neglect, and fail to notice. Sometimes our best intentions will be irrelevant to the damage. Sometimes we will learn, years late, that a joke was a wound or that silence took a side. The wish to be a good person can make these discoveries unbearable. So we defend our identity instead of repairing our effects: *That is not who I am.*

Perhaps not. But the question is not whether your self-image survives intact. The question is what happens next.

Repair has a plain structure:

1. **Name what you did without decorating your intent.** “I interrupted you and dismissed your concern,” not “I'm sorry you felt attacked.”
2. **Acknowledge the effect without demanding that it be minimized.** The harmed person does not owe you immediate absolution.
3. **Make what restitution you can.** Correct the record. Return what was taken. Give credit. Pay the cost. Change the decision if it can be changed.
4. **Alter the conditions that made repetition likely.** An apology with no change is often just a request to be allowed to continue.
5. **Accept some discomfort as yours to carry.** Do not make the person you injured comfort you for feeling guilty.

Repair is not self-hatred. Self-hatred can be another form of self-absorption, keeping the injured person waiting while you perform your remorse. The point is to return attention to where it belongs: to consequences, needs, and changed behavior.

Nor does every apology reopen a door. People are allowed to keep distance. A better world is not one in which everyone must reconcile. It is one in which harm is neither hidden nor endlessly repeated, and in which no one has to pretend to be unhurt to make things comfortable.

## Give help that increases freedom

The best help does not merely relieve pain for an hour. Where possible, it leaves people with more ability to shape their own lives.

This is why “How can I help?” is sometimes less useful than “Would it help if I did this specific thing?” A person in crisis may have no spare energy to design your generosity. Offer something concrete and easy to refuse: a meal on Tuesday, a ride on Thursday, an hour of childcare, help reading a form. Then listen to the answer.

Ask what is actually needed rather than donating what flatters you. Share useful knowledge without using it to control. Lend money carefully, give it when you can, and do not purchase obedience with either. When helping communities you do not belong to, treat local knowledge as evidence, not as an obstacle to your plan. When you can support a change that removes the need for repeated rescue, consider that too.

And include yourself among the people whose suffering is real. Self-neglect is not a reliable source of generosity. Exhausted, frightened people often have less attention for others; chronic self-sacrifice can become resentment with a noble name. Rest, treatment, safety, and honest limits are not betrayals of other people. You are not required to set yourself on fire to prove that someone else is cold.

The aim is not maximum personal sacrifice. It is less avoidable suffering and more shared freedom, sustained over time.

## Refuse the anesthesia of scale

When suffering is vast, the mind offers two escapes: obsession and numbness. Obsession tries to hold every emergency at once until grief becomes paralysis. Numbness decides that because you cannot care about everything, you need care about nothing.

Neither is necessary. You are allowed a finite life and a finite field of responsibility. Choose a few problems large enough to matter and close enough for you to affect. Learn about them. Give regularly if you have money. Work with others rather than relying only on private purity. Vote and organize with attention to consequences for people unlike yourself. Support competent institutions. Check whether your good intentions survive contact with evidence.

Then wash the dishes. Be dependable. Enjoy beauty without treating enjoyment as a crime against the suffering. Joy is not the opposite of concern; it is one of the things concern is for.

Do not let perfection become an excuse for inaction. You will benefit from unjust arrangements you did not design. You will own objects whose histories you cannot fully trace. You will give to the wrong thing, believe the wrong claim, and discover that some choices involve losses on every side. Moral seriousness begins not when you achieve innocence, but when you stop requiring innocence before you will act.

A practical question can cut through the fog: **Who bears the cost of my convenience, and is there a reasonable way to bear a fairer share myself?** Sometimes the answer will require a different purchase. Sometimes a different policy. Sometimes only a pause before speaking. Often you will not know. Then learning, not self-congratulation, is the next step.

## A practice for an ordinary day

You do not need to wait to feel transformed. Try this for one week, not as a performance of virtue but as an experiment in attention.

Once each day, before you act, ask:

- **Who else will live with the effects of this?**
- **What might they know or fear that I have not considered?**
- **Can I achieve what I need with less humiliation, less waste, or less fear?**

Then do one thing within reach.

Return the call you have been avoiding. Give instructions without sarcasm. Let the slower person finish. Correct a rumor before forwarding it. Ask the quiet person what they think, and wait through the silence. Leave the place usable for whoever comes next. Tell someone specifically what their work made possible. Make the complaint without inventing an enemy. Put your phone away while someone tells you the difficult part. If you have influence, use it to make one fair process more fair. If you caused harm, begin repair without bargaining for forgiveness.

Do not expect each act to feel meaningful. Meaning is often visible only from the receiving side. You may never learn that a small courtesy interrupted someone's worst day, or that your willingness to admit error gave a room permission to tell the truth.

You will also fail at this practice. Notice without turning the notice into theater. Begin again at the next available moment.

That moment is closer than it appears. Someone will soon need information, patience, privacy, encouragement, a clear refusal, or simply the relief of not being treated as an object. You will not be able to supply everything. You are not being asked to.

You are being asked not to add casually to the weight.

And where you can, to lift some.

The world will not become gentle merely because you read this. But another person's world can become gentler because of what you do next. That is not a consolation prize. Across billions of days, in kitchens and clinics, offices and streets, courtrooms and comments sections, it is one of the main ways a civilization becomes fit to live in.

Start there. Start with the next person. Let them remain fully human in your mind, and let your conduct make it a little easier for them to remain fully alive in their own.
