# The Next Person You Meet

The driver who cut you off this morning may have been careless. He may also have been rushing to a hospital, half-asleep after a night shift, or lost in a grief so fresh that the road had blurred. You do not know. I do not know. Yet most of us decide in half a second: *idiot*.

The cashier is slow: *lazy*. The colleague sends a short message: *rude*. A stranger online says something foolish: *evil, stupid, beyond hope*.

Our minds are quick storytellers and poor listeners. They take one moment, one sentence, one photograph, and build a whole person out of it — usually a person who exists only to block our path, waste our time, or prove that the world is going wrong.

This essay asks for one small change, small enough to begin before you finish reading it:

**Treat the next person you meet as fully real.**

Not as background in your story. Not as an obstacle, an audience, a customer, an opponent, or a type. As a life that feels, from the inside, as large as yours feels to you.

> The person in front of you is not an interruption to your life. For a moment, your lives are the same event.

That sounds simple. Practised every day, by enough people, it would change homes, workplaces, streets, governments, and the internet. It would not end war, disease, poverty, or injustice by itself. No single idea can do that. But nearly every cruelty begins by forgetting it, and nearly every act of repair begins by remembering.

## 1. They are as real as you are

Behind the face in front of you is a private world you will never fully see.

There is a childhood that shaped them. A body that aches in places they do not mention. Someone they miss. Something they are ashamed of. A hope they are almost embarrassed to say aloud. They have lain awake at 3 a.m. They have waited for a message. They have said the wrong thing and replayed it for years. They are, like you, trying to get through the day with the mixture of courage, confusion, habit, and luck they happen to have.

You cannot carry everyone's suffering. You are not required to like everyone, trust everyone, or let everyone close. Some people are dangerous. Some must be stopped, avoided, reported, or opposed.

But before we decide what to do, we can refuse the first lie: that they are less real than we are.

A useful question is to replace *"What is wrong with them?"* with *"What might be happening that I cannot see?"*

You may never learn the answer. The question is still worthwhile, because it loosens the grip of contempt. And contempt is expensive. It makes punishment feel like pleasure. It makes listening feel unnecessary. It allows us to harm people while feeling clean, because we have already turned them into creatures who deserve it.

Nations do this with whole peoples. Neighbours do it over a fence. Families do it at the dinner table. Strangers do it through a screen, where there is no face to remind us that a life is there.

Reverse it wherever you can, starting at the smallest scale. Give the benefit of the doubt when the doubt is genuinely there. Speak of absent people as if they might one day hear you — not out of fear, but out of respect for the fact that they exist when you are not looking.

## 2. Your first story is only a draft

You will be wrong about people. Often. So will I.

We mistake tiredness for coldness, shyness for arrogance, fear for anger, ignorance for malice. We read a message in the worst possible tone. We remember the one sentence that confirms what we already believed and quietly drop the ten that did not.

Hold your judgments, but hold them lightly.

Ask: *Do I actually know this, or am I guessing? What else could be true? What would change my mind?*

Those questions are not weakness. They are accuracy. Confidence feels strong; accuracy *is* strong. A person who can say "I was wrong" or "I don't know yet" is not smaller than the rest of us. They are one of the few people in the room who can still learn.

This matters most when we are angry, and most of all online. Outrage travels faster than context. A clip is cut, a screenshot is shared, a crowd gathers, and by the time the missing facts arrive, the punishment has already been enjoyed. Before you pass on a story that makes you furious, pause long enough to ask whether you would share it if it were about someone you loved — and whether you know enough to be sure it is true.

Then, when someone is actually in front of you, try listening for a few seconds longer than feels natural. Repeat back what you heard: "It sounds like you felt ignored." You may discover that the argument was never about the dishes, the deadline, or the parking space. Most arguments are about a hidden sentence underneath: *Do I matter to you? Do you respect me? Am I safe?*

You do not have to agree with a person to hear that sentence. You only have to stop arguing with the stranger you invented and start talking to the person who is there.

## 3. Small is not small

We tend to wait for a large life: more money, more power, more time, a better job, a healed world. Then, we tell ourselves, we will be generous and brave.

Meanwhile, the actual world is being made out of tiny, repeated things.

A door held or let fall. A message sent or mercifully deleted. A driver waved in. A junior colleague thanked by name. A piece of rubbish carried to a bin. A rumour repeated — or allowed to die with you. Five honest minutes given to a child who will remember them for years.

None of these will make the news. Multiply one of them by eight billion people, and then by every day of a lifetime, and you have the weather of a civilisation.

We ask, "What difference can I make?" as if the answer must be measured all at once. But trust, safety, and decency are not built in single heroic acts. They are built the way a path is built: by many feet choosing the same direction.

This is not an excuse to ignore laws, institutions, or injustice. "Be kind" is not a substitute for fair pay, clean water, honest courts, or stopping violence. Private goodness and public justice need each other. Do not use a broken system as an excuse for personal cruelty — and do not use personal kindness as an excuse to leave a harmful system untouched. We have two hands. Use both.

Nor is kindness the same as being pleasant. Sometimes kindness tells a hard truth, sets a boundary, refuses to help with something wrong, or says: *Stop. Not here.* It can leave a relationship, call for help, or stand between a bully and the person being bullied. Soft voices can have iron in them. The aim is not to keep everyone comfortable. The aim is to protect what is human in everyone, including yourself.

## 4. Repair faster than you defend

You will fail at all of this. Today, probably.

You will snap at someone who loves you. You will judge too fast, speak too sharply, forget, interrupt, exaggerate, stay silent when you should have spoken. So will the people around you.

The future of a relationship, a family, a workplace — sometimes of a country — depends less on whether mistakes happen than on what happens next. The first harm is often an accident. The second harm is pride: doubling down, rewriting history, attacking the person we hurt for daring to be hurt.

Repair early, while the repair is still small.

A real apology needs only three plain parts:

1. **What I did.** "I raised my voice and walked out."
2. **That it mattered.** "That must have felt frightening and dismissive. I'm sorry."
3. **What I will do.** "Next time I'll say I need ten minutes, and then I'll come back."

Notice what is missing: *if*, *but*, and "I'm sorry you feel that way." Those are not apologies. They are defences wearing an apology's coat.

And when someone repairs toward you, consider meeting them partway. Forgiving does not always mean trusting again, returning, or pretending nothing happened. Sometimes it simply means refusing to feed on revenge — refusing to let the worst thing someone did become the only story either of you is allowed to live in.

Say "I don't know" quickly. Thank people who correct you. Ask for help before resentment makes the asking bitter. The ability to repair is not a personality trait some people are born with. It is a habit, and habits can be practised by anyone, starting badly.

## 5. Leave it cleaner than you found it

Every day, you will leave things behind for people you will never meet: a room, a conversation, a group chat, a road, a stretch of river, a mood in a house, a planet.

Ask one question of all of them: *Will the person who comes after me be glad I was here?*

Wash the dish. Return the tool. Write the clear note instead of the clever, confusing one. Disagree without humiliating. Take your anger somewhere it will not be inherited by an innocent bystander. Use a little less of what is shared. Plant, mend, save, and clean up in the unfashionable places where no one will applaud you.

The people of the future are as real as the person in front of you. They simply cannot knock on the door and ask us to be careful. Everything we leave them — debts and forests, weapons and libraries, habits of hatred and habits of trust — will be the world they wake up in. We are somebody's ancestors, whether or not anyone remembers our names.

Leave people, where you can, with their dignity intact. Leave places, where you can, a little more alive.

## A practice for today, not someday

You do not need a new personality, a retreat, or a perfect morning routine. Take the next ordinary interaction — at home, at work, in a shop, in traffic, on a screen — and try five things:

1. **Pause.** One slow breath before you answer, send, honk, post, or conclude. That breath is the space where a better action becomes possible.
2. **Trade the label for a question.** Not *jerk, liar, hopeless* — but *What is going on here? What am I missing?*
3. **Say the useful thing.** True, kind, and needed. If a sentence is only one of the three, consider silence: truth without kindness can be cruelty, kindness without truth can be a lie, and neither is useful if this is not the moment it is needed.
4. **Repair before the day ends**, if it is safe to do so. One honest message is enough to begin: "I've been thinking about what I said. I'm sorry. Can we talk?"
5. **Leave one shared thing better.** Pick up the litter. Send the thank-you. Credit the person who did the work. Make the note clearer. Let someone go first.

That is all. It will not feel like changing the world. Do it again tomorrow anyway.

## Begin with whoever is nearest

If everyone read an essay and then waited for everyone else to change first, nothing would happen. The point of reading is smaller and more demanding: *I will go first, in the next ten minutes, with the person nearest to me.*

You may never know what it prevents. The message you do not send may be the one that would have ended a friendship. The question you ask instead of the judgment you deliver may be the first time someone has felt heard in months. The apology you make today may interrupt a resentment that would otherwise have been handed down for years, like furniture nobody wanted but everybody kept.

And when you turn this way of seeing toward the last person — the one in the mirror — let it work there too. You are also more than your worst moment, your laziest label, your most frightened story about yourself. Be as honest with yourself as honesty requires, and as merciful as you would try to be with anyone else. Self-contempt has never made a person more truthful, braver, or kinder. It only makes the world one person poorer.

We do not need to agree on everything to share a world. We do not need to become saints, heroes, or optimists. We need enough of us, often enough, to remember in the moment of friction that the other person is real, that our first story may be wrong, that small things multiply, that repair is strength, and that someone will live with what we leave behind.

So begin there.

Not with humanity in general, which is too large to love all at once.

With the next person you meet.
