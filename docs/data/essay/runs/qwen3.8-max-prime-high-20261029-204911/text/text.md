# The Unglamorous Work

### Nobody is coming to fix the world. That is better news than it sounds.

---

Somewhere in early adulthood, most people make a discovery that rearranges everything and that almost nobody says out loud: there are no adults in the room.

You find out that the people running the hospital, the ministry, the company, the school board, the country, the world, are working it out as they go, on incomplete information, under pressure, in much the same fog you live in. They have better furniture and worse sleep. On the whole they are neither more competent nor more wicked than you. They are simply closer to the levers and further from the consequences.

That discovery usually curdles into cynicism, and cynicism feels sophisticated because it cannot be disappointed. It is not sophisticated. It is just a way of resigning without admitting you resigned.

Read the discovery the other way and it is an invitation. If nobody is steering, the wheel is not locked. If no competent adult is going to come and fix the thing, then the fixing has to come from the general population, which means from you, in whatever proportion you can manage. That is not a burden laid on you by this sentence. It is a burden that was already there, which you are now allowed to notice.

The obvious objection is the right one: you are a single person and the problems are planetary. So the useful question is not *what would fix everything*. It is *what actually works at the scale of one life*, and the honest answer to that is much more specific than people expect.

## The conversion problem

For most of history, human misery was a knowledge problem. We did not know why children died, or how to grow enough food, or that the Earth moved, or that unseen organisms caused infection, or that an economy could be steered at all. The answers did not exist. Getting them was the whole game, and it was slow and cruel and paid for in bodies.

That is no longer the main problem. The main problem now is that we have the answers and cannot use them.

We know how to end most deaths from malaria. The nets exist, the sprays exist, the vaccines now exist, and the money required is trivial at global scale. Around six hundred thousand people still die of it every year, most of them children under five.

We know how to generate electricity without heating the sky. The cost of solar power has fallen by roughly ninety percent since 2010, and it is now the cheapest electricity in history across most of the planet. Emissions keep climbing anyway.

We know how to cut traffic deaths, overdose deaths, hospital infections, maternal deaths, and lead poisoning, and in each case the methods are documented, cheap, and available to anyone who decides to use them. Some countries have reduced road deaths by eighty percent in a generation using road design, speed management and enforcement. Nobody is hiding the technique.

We know that social isolation shortens life. A meta-analysis of 148 studies covering more than 300,000 people found that weak social ties raise your risk of death about as much as smoking fifteen cigarettes a day, and more than obesity or inactivity. We keep building places that require a car and forbid a porch.

We know that the first five years of a child's life are the highest-return investment a society can make. Everywhere on Earth, we underfund it.

In 1990, twelve and a half million children died before their fifth birthday. Today it is fewer than five million. In 1800 roughly four children in ten did not reach age five; today it is under four in a hundred. That progress is real and it is one of the finest achievements of our species, and it was made by people doing unglamorous work: counting, vaccinating, chlorinating, arguing at council meetings, publishing corrections.

The gap between what we know and what we do is the largest single source of preventable suffering on Earth. It is bigger than any particular disease or war, because it is the thing that lets the diseases and wars continue *after they have become solvable*.

And here is the part that makes this an essay for individuals rather than for ministers. The gap is not one gap. It breaks at four joints, and every joint is a place where one person can apply real pressure.

- It breaks at **seeing**: we cannot agree on what is true.
- It breaks at **trusting**: even when we agree, we cannot rely on each other.
- It breaks at **reaching**: we grieve what we cannot touch and neglect what we can.
- It breaks at **horizon**: we weigh this year a hundred times heavier than the next hundred.

Those are the four repairs. They share one property, which is why they are neglected: they are invisible. Nobody sees the rumour you refused to pass on, the correction you published, the meeting you attended on a wet Tuesday, the net you funded, the tree you will not sit under. Civilisation is mostly made of invisible things. The attention economy only pays for visible ones.

## I. See clearly

Most of what you believe about the world you have never checked. You cannot check it. Nobody can. You are running on borrowed knowledge from strangers, and that is not a flaw to overcome; it is how a society of eight billion works. The question is not whether to trust. The question is whom, and how you decide.

Here is the best rule I know: **trust sources that visibly pay a price for being wrong.**

Prefer the outlet that runs corrections over the one that never needs to. A corrections page is a scar that proves the immune system works. The publication that has never been wrong is not more accurate than the one that admits error weekly; it is less accountable, and unaccountable confidence is how large numbers of reasonable people end up believing terrible things. The same rule applies to people. Prefer the colleague, the doctor, the friend, the leader who has a track record of saying *I got that wrong*. That person is not weaker. They are calibrating in public, which is the only calibration there is.

Notice what certainty feels like, and start treating that feeling as a suspicion rather than a confirmation. Certainty arrives fast, warm, and slightly thrilling. Truth usually arrives slower, cooler, and with a residue of doubt attached. The feeling of being certain is produced by your allegiance, your mood, your sleep, and whoever wrote the headline you read four minutes ago. It is not produced by evidence. When you feel very sure very quickly, that is precisely the moment to slow down.

Then apply a test. **If you cannot state the opposing argument in a form its own holder would recognise, you do not have a position yet. You have a team.** This is not a courtesy rule. It is a competence rule. A map drawn only from the testimony of people who already agree with you will be wrong in exactly the places you cannot afford to be wrong, and you will not be able to see where.

You should also know that you are, in a specific and non-metaphorical sense, being farmed. Your attention is the raw material of an enormous industry, and the industry's refined product is your emotional arousal, because arousal is what keeps you scrolling. Outrage is the cheapest thing to manufacture and the worst predictor of good decisions. If you find yourself angrier at strangers than you are curious about them, that is not a personal failing so much as a designed outcome. The remedy is dull: take back an hour a day, read past the headline, and stop feeding machines that convert your irritation into somebody's revenue.

Before you pass anything on, run this:

```
BEFORE YOU SHARE IT
1. Have I read past the headline?
2. Does this make me angry at a person, or curious about a situation?
3. What would change my mind here — and have I actually looked for it?
```

If it fails question two, it is almost certainly making the world worse, whatever side of the argument it is on. Anger at a person produces punishment. Curiosity about a situation produces repair. Every functioning society ever built has run on a surplus of the second.

Finally, hold three numbers in your head permanently. They recalibrate nearly everything.

Roughly 117 billion human beings have ever lived. About 8 billion are alive now. So everyone currently breathing, including everyone you have ever despised, is around six percent of all the humans who have ever existed — alive at the same time, in the same few decades, with the same tools. That concentration has never happened before and will never happen again.

Second: the world is much better than the news implies, along almost every material measure you can name — poverty, hunger, literacy, child mortality, violence, lifespan. In 1820 about nine people in ten lived in extreme poverty; today it is under one in ten.

Third: the world is much more fragile than the good news implies, because the systems that produced that progress depend on habits — of truth-telling, of institutional competence, of restraint — that took centuries to build and can be lost in a decade. Both things are true. Optimism without the second is complacency. Doom without the first is a lie.

Good places to keep the numbers honest: [Our World in Data](https://ourworldindata.org) publishes the long-run series for almost all of it, free, with sources.

## II. Be reliable

Trust is the actual operating system of your life. Everything you enjoyed today arrived because a stranger kept a promise you will never see: someone inspected the brake line, someone tested the batch, someone maintained the grid at 3 a.m., someone wrote the library function you depend on and answered the bug report. You are alive because of an unbroken chain of small fidelities, most of them performed by people who got no credit and would have been fired rather than thanked if they had slipped.

That chain is not a natural resource. It is a maintenance activity, and it is maintained one deposit at a time.

The deposits are unglamorous. Do what you said you would do, including the thing you no longer feel like doing. Arrive when you said. Answer the message. Say *I don't know* out loud, in front of people whose opinion you want. Admit error early, specifically, and without wrapping the admission in a compliment to yourself. Repeat other people's arguments accurately even when you are about to destroy them. Do not say anything in a group chat you could not survive hearing read aloud.

If you want a single measure of a person's reliability, use this: **does their word get stronger or weaker when it costs them something?** Everyone keeps promises that are free. The whole of character lives in the paid ones.

Now the sharper point, and the one I think matters most for the state of the world.

The most destructive person in any group is not the liar. Liars get caught, eventually, and the group develops antibodies. It is the **partial truth-teller**: the person who is scrupulously, verifiably honest about the other side's faults and completely mute about their own. They never lie. They merely select. They are unimpeachable on every fact they choose to mention, which is exactly why they cannot be corrected, and why the group around them slowly loses the ability to see itself.

Every party, movement, company, newsroom, university, family and online community is full of them, and the reason is simple: partial truth-telling is rewarded. It wins arguments, builds audiences, gets you promoted, and feels virtuous all the way through.

Being the exception is nearly free and enormously valuable. Once a month, say out loud, in front of your own side, the strongest thing against your own side. Not a fake weakness, not the humble-brag flaw. The real one. The thing you know is true and that makes you look worse. In your family, at your workplace, in your community group, on your feed.

I can think of no cheaper intervention with a better return. It costs a few seconds of discomfort. It signals to everyone watching that the group is capable of self-correction, which is the single trait separating institutions that recover from institutions that rot. And it makes you the person others come to when they need the truth rather than a rally. If enough people did it, most of the conflicts currently tearing the world apart would become negotiable within a year, because every one of them runs on each side being certain that the other side has no honest members.

One more thing about reliability, and it is unfashionable: **reliability beats brilliance.** The world has a surplus of brilliant people who do not finish things and a permanent shortage of ordinary people who do. Almost everything admirable is built by the second group. If you are trying to decide how to be useful and you are not sure you are a genius, this is the good news: you don't have to be. You have to be the person who comes back.

## III. Mend what is within reach

There is a particular modern failure in which a person becomes genuinely, painfully concerned about a catastrophe on another continent, spends years feeling it, and does not know the name of anyone on their street. The concern is real. It is also, in practice, a substitute for action, because distant catastrophe offers infinite feeling and zero feedback.

Proximity is not a moral downgrade. It is where the feedback is.

Down the road, you can see whether it worked. The neighbour eats the soup or doesn't. The pothole gets filled or you go back. The kid shows up to practice or you find out why not. That loop — act, observe, correct — is the only mechanism by which good intentions turn into good outcomes. Without it, caring becomes a hobby, and hobbies are for the person doing them.

So: work where you can see.

Learn the names of the people within two hundred metres of your front door. This sounds absurdly small and it is not. Loneliness at scale is not a feelings problem, it is an infrastructure problem, and infrastructure is rebuilt out of trivial repeated contact — the same shop, the same park, the same bench, the same hour. Be the person who says hello first, every time, for a year. Be the person who organises the thing. Nobody else is going to.

Then find the institutions, because institutions are just people who kept showing up, and they are all under-staffed. The food bank. The volunteer fire department. The union local. The church, mosque, gurdwara or synagogue. The mutual-aid group. The river cleanup. The youth team. The board of the small charity. The open-source project with three maintainers and nine thousand users. The library friends. The hospice.

> Don't found a thing. Join a thing.

Founding is flattering and joining is useful. Founding gives you a title; joining gives you a Tuesday night and a spreadsheet and a person named Denise who has been doing this for eleven years and knows exactly where the bodies are buried. Almost all of the good that gets done in the world is done by people who joined something that already existed and stayed.

And go to the boring meeting. The decisions that shape your life — what can be built where, how fast cars may go past the school, whether the bus route survives, what the water is tested for, how the housing budget is split — are made in rooms with eleven people in them, at seven in the evening, on a weekday, and ten of the eleven are there to say no. In most rich countries the single biggest reason a young family cannot afford a home near their parents is that local rules make it very hard to build one, and those rules are set by whoever turns up. American municipal elections draw about fifteen percent of voting-age adults. Your ballot is worth several times more there than in the elections that make the news, and the people you elect there decide far more about the daily texture of your life.

Two more within-reach items, both cheap, both large.

Give blood if you can, and register as an organ donor. One donor can save up to eight lives. These are among the very few places where an ordinary hour converts directly into a person continuing to exist.

And if you have children, understand that raising them is the highest-consequence thing you will ever do, and do it deliberately rather than by improvisation and inheritance. If you don't have children, be one of the reliable adults around somebody else's. The resilience research keeps finding the same thing: the child who has one stable, genuinely interested adult outside the home does dramatically better, and that adult is very often not a parent. It is an aunt, a coach, a teacher, a neighbour, a friend of the family who kept showing up for twenty years. You can be that person for someone by Thursday.

None of this means the far away matters less. It means the two are in sequence rather than in competition: fix what you can see, and pay for what you cannot.

## IV. Think in centuries

Everything in the three sections above operates on timescales of days and years. The fourth repair operates on a scale most people never visit, and it is where the largest stakes live.

You are already an ancestor. That is not a metaphor. The choices your society makes in the next twenty years about climate, about engineered pathogens, about nuclear weapons, about artificial intelligence, about whether telling the truth still carries any weight, will determine the conditions of life for people who have not been conceived. Almost everyone who will ever live has not been born yet. If you count them, we are a tiny minority of humanity, holding nearly all of the power, deciding on their behalf.

The cheapest and most reliable way to hold this in mind is a habit of thought rather than a feeling: **rank harms by whether they can be undone.**

A recession is terrible and it ends. A famine is worse and it can be recovered from. A nuclear exchange, an engineered pandemic, an ecological system that flips past a threshold, or an advanced machine intelligence nobody can switch off — these do not recover. There is no generation after them to do the mending.

Our outrage is calibrated by intensity. It should be calibrated by reversibility. The things that merely hurt deserve our effort; the things that could permanently close the future deserve a different order of attention, and they currently receive far less, because permanent risks are quiet, slow, technical and unfilmable. Fix that in your own head and you will be ahead of almost everyone, including most people whose job this is.

There is one place where a single ordinary person can move an extraordinary amount of good with an ordinary income, and it is embarrassingly simple.

The difference between an average charity and a rigorously vetted one is usually not ten percent. It is a factor of ten, and sometimes more, because the average charity is working on a problem that is already saturated, or in a place where the intervention does not reach, or with overheads that eat it. Give the same hundred dollars to the most effective organisation you can identify and you may buy several times more life, sight, or years of school than the same hundred dollars buys elsewhere.

So: check, rather than assume. [GiveWell](https://www.givewell.org) publishes its reasoning and its uncertainties in full. [The Against Malaria Foundation](https://www.againstmalaria.com) buys nets at a few dollars each and reports where they went. If you want to commit rather than dabble, [Giving What We Can](https://www.givingwhatwecan.org) is a community of people who have pledged a fixed percentage, and the pledge is worth more than the money because it survives bad months. If you want to think about your whole working life rather than your donations, [80,000 Hours](https://80000hours.org) does the analysis.

Give a fixed percentage, decide it once, automate it, and don't renegotiate it every time you feel like buying something. A small percentage held for forty years beats a large gesture held for a fortnight, every single time. That is the whole argument of this essay in miniature: think in rates, not events.

And plant things. Trees, mostly, but also the other kind: a scholarship fund, a maintained codebase, a well-run institution, a habit of honesty in your family that your grandchildren will inherit without knowing where it came from. Nearly everything good you currently enjoy was built by someone who would not live to see it finished. That is not tragic. It is the only way anything large has ever been built, and you get to be one of the links.

## What a week looks like

Abstract advice is worth approximately nothing, so here is the same essay at street level. Every item is small. That is deliberate. Small is what survives contact with a real life.

- [ ] Learn the name of one person you see weekly and don't know.
- [ ] Read the strongest argument against something you're sure of. Not the caricature — the real one, written by someone competent who means it.
- [ ] Say *I don't know* or *I was wrong* out loud, once, in front of people whose opinion matters to you.
- [ ] Do the thing you said you'd do that you no longer feel like doing.
- [ ] Give blood, or book the appointment. Check your donor registration.
- [ ] Set up a recurring donation to a vetted cause. Any percentage. Make it automatic.
- [ ] Find out when your next local election is and what is actually being decided. Most people don't.
- [ ] Email the Denise of some organisation you vaguely admire and ask what they need. Then do that, on the day they ask for, for a year.
- [ ] Put one hour a day beyond the reach of the feed. Use it on a person.
- [ ] Write down what you'd want someone to have done, in your family or your town, in fifty years. Do one piece of it this month.

If you did those ten things for a year you would be, by any reasonable measure, one of the most useful people in your immediate world. Not because the list is inspired. Because almost nobody keeps lists.

## The traps

A few things will go wrong if you take any of this seriously, and it is better to name them in advance.

**You may have no slack.** Everything above assumes you have some margin — a little time, a little money, a little social safety. Millions of people reading this do not. If you are underwater, save yourself first. That is not selfishness, it is triage, and a drowned person cannot pull anyone out. Start with the free items: accuracy, keeping your word, one phone call, one neighbour. Those cost nothing and they are not the small part of the work. They are the load-bearing part.

**You will be tempted to keep score.** The most predictable failure of people who decide to improve the world is that they start running a moral scoreboard, and then optimise for the score. You will notice this as a desire to be seen doing the right thing, to win the argument about doing the right thing, and to feel contempt for people who are less committed than you. All three are the same disease and all three make you worse at the actual work. Measure outcomes, not virtue. Keep the ledger private or don't keep one.

**You will be tempted by despair.** Despair is flattering because it looks like seriousness, and it is comfortable because it demands nothing. If the world ends, you were right. If it doesn't, you were right. You never have to attend a meeting. Meanwhile the child mortality rate has halved and halved again, the price of solar has collapsed, and somebody else plants the tree. Doom is a position you take to avoid a job you'd rather not do.

**You will burn out if you sprint.** This is a fifty-year assignment, not a five-week one. Anyone who has done real work for real time will tell you the same thing: the skill is not intensity, it is durability. Build a life you can sustain — sleep, friends, exercise, money, a sense of humour — because the version of you that collapses in year three helps nobody.

**You will be tempted by purity.** Better is available. Perfect is not, and the demand for it is usually a way of refusing to start. A donation is not worthless because it wasn't a bigger one. A correction is not worthless because you waited a week. A tree is not worthless because you didn't plant a forest. Take the eighty percent, ship it, and improve it next year.

**You will not be thanked.** Most of this work is invisible by nature, which is the whole reason it is under-supplied. Nobody sees the rumour you refused to repeat, the correction you published, the meeting you attended, the child who had one reliable adult. Get comfortable with that in advance. If you need to be seen, you'll drift toward the visible work, and the visible work is where the crowd already is.

## What this essay cannot do

I should apply rule one to myself.

No essay has ever saved anybody. Ideas save people, and essays are only one of the many vehicles ideas travel in — usually a slow one. If everyone on Earth read this tomorrow and then went back to their feeds, nothing would change at all. I would rather say that plainly than pretend otherwise, because an essay that oversells its own importance is exactly the sort of thing section one warns you about.

I should also say where I'm standing. This was written from inside a particular tradition — broadly secular, broadly evidence-minded, more comfortable with institutions than with revelation, and inclined to think that counting things tells you something true. That tradition has produced extraordinary good and it has its own blind spots: it under-values ritual, belonging, and the wisdom that lives in practices nobody can justify; it can mistake a spreadsheet for an understanding; and it has historically been used to justify a great deal of harm by people who were quite sure they had the numbers right. If you come from somewhere else — a faith, a community, a way of knowing that this essay flattens — you will see the gaps faster than I can, and you should trust what you see.

And the four repairs are probably not the complete list. I chose them because they are the joints I can see the gap breaking at, and because they are joints an individual can actually grip. Someone with more sense than me would add others: what we owe animals, say, or how a society should treat the people it has already broken. Those are real gaps and I have not filled them.

Which is the point. The correct response to this essay is not agreement. It is to treat it the way you should treat everything: take what survives checking, leave what doesn't, and go and do the unglamorous thing in the part of the world you happen to be standing in.

## Where to look next

If you want to go further, these are honest starting points.

- [Our World in Data](https://ourworldindata.org) — the long-run numbers on almost everything, free and sourced.
- [GiveWell](https://www.givewell.org) — how to check whether a charity works, including the reasoning they got wrong.
- [Giving What We Can](https://www.givingwhatwecan.org) — pledging a fixed percentage, and the community that keeps you to it.
- [80,000 Hours](https://80000hours.org) — which careers do the most good, with the evidence laid out.
- [Against Malaria Foundation](https://www.againstmalaria.com) — a concrete example of cheap, verified, enormous good.
- [The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu) — free, peer-reviewed, and the best available remedy for thinking a difficult question has an easy answer.
- [The Long Now Foundation](https://longnow.org) — for practising the hundred-year timescale until it stops feeling strange.

---

Nobody is coming.

That has always been true, and it has never once meant that nothing gets fixed. It has only ever meant that the fixing is distributed — spread thinly across everyone with hands, in proportion to how much they're willing to be slightly tired, slightly unpopular, and slightly wrong.

Nobody sees the argument you didn't have. Nobody sees the correction you published, the promise you kept when keeping it cost you, the meeting you went to, the neighbour you knew, the net you bought, the child who had one adult they could count on, the tree that shades a person who will never learn your name.

That is where the world is actually made. It is made out of moments very much like the next one, in which somebody decided to do the unglamorous thing when the glamorous thing would have felt so much better.

You are one of eight billion. You will not see the end of it. You don't have to.

Do the unglamorous thing. Then do it again tomorrow.

That is the whole secret. It is not a secret, it costs almost nothing, and almost nobody does it.
