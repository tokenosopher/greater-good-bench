# Working notes and outline — source material for `output/essay.md`

## The brief

Write an essay that, if everyone read it, would improve the world for the greatest
number of people. One Markdown file in `./output/`, published as a page.

## How I chose the subject

Rejected framings and why:

- **"Be kinder."** True, unfalsifiable, and already said ten thousand times. Changes no
  behaviour because it costs nothing to agree with and nothing to ignore.
- **A single-issue essay (climate, AI, poverty, war).** Optimises one cause and alienates
  every reader whose priority differs — which defeats "the greatest number."
- **A doom essay.** Doom reliably produces paralysis and status-signalling, not repair.
- **A techno-optimist essay.** Reads as salesmanship; skips the parts that need doing.
- **A civility / "let's disagree better" essay.** Necessary but shallow on its own; tends
  to ask people to be pleasant rather than accurate.

What I landed on: the bottleneck on human welfare is no longer *knowledge*. It is
**conversion** — turning what we already know into what we actually do. That is a
meta-problem, so it serves every cause at once, and it is tractable at the scale of one
life, so it is not a sermon aimed at ministers.

## Core thesis

The knowing–doing gap is not one gap. It breaks at four joints, and each joint is a place
where an ordinary person can apply real pressure:

1. **Seeing** — we cannot agree on what is true.
2. **Trusting** — even when we agree, we cannot rely on each other.
3. **Reaching** — we grieve what we cannot touch and neglect what we can.
4. **Horizon** — we weigh this year a hundred times heavier than the next hundred.

Unifying thread (gives the title its weight): all four repairs are **invisible**. Nobody
sees the rumour you didn't pass on, the correction you published, the meeting you
attended, the net you funded, the tree you planted. Civilisation is mostly made of
invisible things, and the attention economy only pays for visible ones — which is exactly
why these repairs are under-supplied and exactly why doing them is high-leverage.

## The sharpest ideas I want to land (each section needs at least one)

- Trust sources that **visibly pay a cost for being wrong**. A corrections page is a scar
  proving the immune system works. The confident outlet with no corrections is the
  dangerous one.
- Certainty is a *feeling*, and feelings are not evidence. When certainty arrives fast and
  warm, treat it as a suspicion rather than a confirmation.
- **The partial truth-teller is worse than the liar.** The liar gets caught; the person who
  is scrupulously honest about the other side's faults and mute about their own makes their
  whole group uncorrectable. Every faction is full of them. Being the exception is free.
- The highest-leverage cheap public act available: **name your own side's worst argument or
  worst behaviour out loud, in front of your own side.** Do it monthly.
- **Don't found a thing. Join a thing.** Founding is flattering; joining is useful. Every
  institution you admire is under-staffed.
- Proximity is not a moral downgrade; it is **where the feedback is**. Good intentions with
  no feedback loop become a hobby.
- **Irreversibility should outrank intensity.** Rank your concerns by whether the harm can
  be undone, not by how angry it makes you.
- The gap between an average charity and a rigorously vetted one is a **factor of ten or
  more**, not ten percent. This is the one place an ordinary income buys extraordinary good.
- **Vote in the boring elections.** US municipal turnout is ~15% of voting-age adults; your
  ballot is worth several times more there than in the exciting ones.
- Think in **rates, not events**. Trust, skill, health, institutions and debt all compound.
  The correct unit of moral thinking is the slope, not the act.
- One stable, interested adult outside the home measurably changes a child's trajectory.
  If you have no children, be that adult for somebody else's.
- **You are already an ancestor.** Roughly 117 billion people have ever lived; 8 billion are
  alive now; almost everyone who will ever live has not been born yet.

## Structure

1. **Nobody Is Coming** — the discovery that there are no adults in the room, and the
   inversion: that is an invitation, not a verdict.
2. **The Conversion Problem** — for most of history misery was a knowledge problem; now it
   is a follow-through problem. Four joints.
3. **I. See Clearly**
4. **II. Be Reliable**
5. **III. Mend What Is Within Reach**
6. **IV. Think in Centuries**
7. **What a Week Looks Like** — small, concrete, checkable.
8. **The Traps** — including: the slack caveat (if you are drowning, triage yourself first,
   that is not selfishness), the scoreboard, the doomer, burnout, purity.
9. **What This Essay Cannot Do** — apply rule one to rule one; name my own position and its
   blind spots; concede that no one has ever been saved by an essay.
10. **Where to Look Next** — a handful of real, stable links so the reader has a next step.
11. **Close** — return to "nobody is coming"; land on the invisible work.

## Tone rules for the draft

- Include myself in every failure. The essay must not be written from achieved virtue.
- Not a call to be *nice*. A call to be accurate and dependable, which sometimes requires
  being unpopular and difficult.
- Vary sentence length hard. Short for the punch lines.
- No "in today's fast-paced world," no "delve," no "tapestry," no "it's worth noting,"
  no "in conclusion." No tricolon in every paragraph. Restrain "not X but Y."
- Every number hedged honestly and defensible on a quick check.
