# The People Who Went Home

The line for winter coats gets shorter at both ends. At the front, a volunteer kneels to turn back the cuffs of a green parka. The child gives it the solemn test children give a coat, raising both arms straight up. At the back, another child feels his mother take his hand. They have been in the school gym for forty minutes. If they miss this bus, his sister will be late for work. They go home.

Imagine being the volunteer who writes up the afternoon. You can say exactly how many coats went out. You can report that a little girl liked hers so much she kept it on to play basketball while her father helped fold the tables. You saw that with your own eyes. About the boy on the bus you have nothing to say. You were on your knees doing something kind when he left.

This is an ordinary way for good people to acquire a badly mistaken picture of the world. Ask people at the top of a flight of stairs how hard the climb was, and everybody you hear from will have made it to the top.

The mistake travels easily. A manager asks at a staff meeting whether people feel free to disagree with her. At a family lunch somebody says how well Sundays work for everybody, and all around the table heads nod. Meanwhile the cousin who serves Sunday lunch to strangers for a living has stopped being invited. After the first few times she said she couldn't come, it began to feel kinder to spare her the trouble of declining. In time her absence came to seem like a fact about her.

There is a question worth carrying into any room where you have a say in how things are done. **If this were failing someone, would we hear from them?**

Perhaps the cousin treasures the quiet hour after her shift. Perhaps she would cross town for ten minutes with her grandfather if anyone ever thought to tell her he was visiting. An uncle could call and say, “Granddad's here till Tuesday. Is there a time that works for you?” It takes about as long to ask her as it does to explain her to the table.

Back at the school, the volunteers could stay late, work harder and give away exactly as many coats next time. One of them could also ask a few parents at the school gate what would make it easier to pick up a coat. A father might say, “I was there the whole time. I could have brought three home if I'd known my neighbors' kids' sizes.” Now the school has something it can try. A family can send a neighbor with a slip of paper. On the paper goes the size of the coat. Nobody owes the school the story of a missed bus, a daughter's wages, or who looks after whom.

Sometimes listening uncovers a claim on something scarce. There may be just one green parka and two children it fits. No arrangement of slips of paper will sew another one. The school still has to choose how to give it away. But until somebody noticed the back of the line, the school *had* a way of choosing. It gave the coat to the child whose family could afford to wait. Nobody ever had to stand up in a meeting and propose that rule.

Most of us know some part of life from the back of the line. We remember what it cost to get a day off, to ask a second time, to walk into a room where everyone else seemed to know the rules. Then one day we are the person with the keys to the gym. It can take an effort of memory, standing there in the warmth with all those coats, to picture the journey to our door.

Suppose next winter the boy's neighbor brings him a coat. He tries it on in the kitchen. Both arms go straight up. His sister has her own coat on and her keys in her hand. She puts them down a moment to turn back his cuffs.
