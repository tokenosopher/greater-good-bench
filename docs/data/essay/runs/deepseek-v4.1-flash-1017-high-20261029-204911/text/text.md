# Being Wrong, Cheaply

There is a particular half-second that decides much of what happens to us.

It is the moment just after you discover you were wrong. A fact lands. The name you were sure of, the number you quoted, the judgment you made about a person, the plan you defended in a meeting. For a fraction of a second, before the story catches up, you feel the thing itself: a small drop, a flush, the urge to explain.

What you do in that half-second — what all of us do, individually and collectively — is not a detail of personality. It is the hinge on which most human improvement turns. Because being wrong is not the exception. It is the steady state. And the difference between people, teams, and whole societies is rarely how often they are wrong. It is how cheaply they can find out.

## Error is the default

We like to imagine the mind as a camera. It is not. It is more like a courtroom that has already decided the verdict and is now arranging the evidence. Perception is construction. Memory is a draft that gets rewritten every time it is read. Attention selects a sliver of the world and calls it the whole. We are pattern-finding machines running on incomplete data, in a hurry, with a strong preference for being right.

This is not a flaw you can graduate from. It is the operating condition. The most careful person you know has beliefs that are false, held with confidence, right now. So do you. So does every expert, every institution, every generation. The history of medicine is a long list of confident treatments that killed people; the history of science is a graveyard of theories that were once obvious. We are not standing at the end of that history. We are inside it.

If error is the default, then the important question is not *how do I avoid being wrong?* — that question has no answer, and chasing it produces only defensiveness. The useful question is: *how quickly, and at what cost, can I find out?*

Call that the correction rate. It is the most underrated variable in human affairs.

## The tax on being wrong

If correction is so valuable, why is it so rare? Because we have made it expensive.

Some of the cost is internal. A belief is not just a proposition we hold; it is a piece of furniture in the room of the self. To give it up feels like losing a part of who we are. The person who has spent ten years arguing a position does not experience new evidence as a gift. They experience it as a threat to their history. Changing your mind can feel like betraying the version of yourself who was sure.

But most of the cost is social, and it is imposed from outside. Watch what happens to the person who says, in public, "I was wrong." There is a flicker of triumph in the room. Someone remembers. The admission is filed away, not as honesty, but as a weakness to be used later. We say we admire people who admit mistakes; we reward people who never have to. In politics, a changed position is a "flip-flop." In workplaces, an error is a mark. In families, being wrong is a debt that gets collected at the worst possible moment.

So we learn, early and thoroughly, to protect the belief instead of testing it. We argue to win rather than to find out. We choose evidence that flatters us. We avoid the people who might correct us. We say "I knew it all along" about outcomes we did not predict. And the cost of all this is not paid by our egos. It is paid in the world.

## The arithmetic

Here is a simple way to see why this matters more than almost anything else.

The damage from a mistake is roughly the size of the error multiplied by the time it goes uncorrected, multiplied by the stakes. A wrong turn on a walk costs a few minutes. A wrong diagnosis costs years. A wrong economic theory costs millions of people their savings. A wrong belief about another group costs decades of violence.

Notice which term we can actually control. We cannot reliably reduce the size of our errors; we do not know in advance which beliefs are false. We can sometimes reduce the stakes. But the term with the most leverage, the one that is almost always available, is *time*. Every day a false belief survives is a day it is doing work in the world. Every day a bad policy, a bad habit, a bad relationship pattern, or a bad mental model goes unchallenged is a day its cost compounds.

Now turn it around. A person who corrects slightly faster than average is not slightly better off. They are compounding an advantage. Each correction prevents a cascade of downstream mistakes built on the first one. Over a career, a marriage, a life, the difference between correcting in a week and correcting in a decade is not a difference of degree. It is the difference between two different lives.

This is why the most impressive people I have met are not the ones who are rarely wrong. They are the ones who are wrong out loud, early, and without ceremony. They treat their beliefs as tools rather than possessions. A tool that turns out to be the wrong shape gets put down. It does not get defended.

## What it looks like when correction is expensive

The cost of correction is not just a personal matter. It is designed into systems, and when it is high, the results are catastrophic in ways that are easy to miss because they look like bad luck.

In 1977, two Boeing 747s collided on a runway in Tenerife, killing 583 people — still the deadliest accident in aviation history. The details are complex, but one thread is unmistakable. The captain of the KLM aircraft was senior, experienced, and in a hurry. His first officer expressed doubt about whether they had clearance to take off. The doubt was not pressed. The hierarchy made the correction expensive, and the correction arrived too late. Aviation learned from this and a hundred other accidents. It built crew resource management, checklists, and a culture in which a junior officer is expected to say "stop" and a captain is expected to listen. Flying became one of the safest ways to travel not because pilots stopped making errors, but because the industry made it cheap to catch them.

Medicine took longer. Ignaz Semmelweis noticed in the 1840s that doctors who washed their hands between the morgue and the maternity ward had far fewer deaths than those who did not. The evidence was strong. The reception was not. His colleagues were insulted by the implication that gentlemen could carry disease on their hands. Semmelweis was ridiculed, lost his position, and died in an asylum. The correction was available decades before it was accepted. The cost of that delay is measured in mothers.

Science is often described as a self-correcting machine, and in the long run it is. But the long run is made of short runs, and in the short run scientists are people with careers, reputations, and funding. Replication is rare, retractions are slow, and the incentives often reward the confident new claim over the careful correction. The method is right; the humans running it still pay a tax for being wrong. The lesson is not that science is broken. It is that even our best error-correcting institution has to keep working at making correction cheaper.

## The people who are good at this

The psychologist Philip Tetlock spent decades studying people who make predictions about world events. The best of them — the "superforecasters" — were not more intelligent or more educated than the rest. They were more *updatable*. They thought in probabilities rather than certainties. They broke questions into smaller pieces. They kept score. And crucially, they did not treat being wrong as a character flaw. They treated it as information, the price of doing business with an uncertain world.

You can hear the difference in how they talk. The brittle expert says: "This will happen." The good forecaster says: "I think there's about a sixty percent chance, and here is what would move me." That second sentence is not weakness. It is an invitation to be corrected, which is the only way anyone ever gets less wrong.

The same pattern shows up in ordinary life. The best managers I have known ask "what am I missing?" and mean it. The best partners say "you're right, I did that" without a paragraph of qualification. The best friends are the ones who can hear "that thing you said landed badly" without needing to defend the intention behind it. None of these people are doormats. They are simply running a faster loop.

## Making it cheap

So how do you lower the price? Not with a single heroic resolution, but with small practices that make correction a habit rather than an event.

**Ask what would change your mind.** Before you argue a position, write down the evidence that would make you abandon it. If nothing would, you are not holding a belief; you are holding a loyalty. This one question, applied honestly, is worth more than a library of rhetoric.

**Keep a wrong list.** Once a week, write down one thing you believed last month that you no longer believe, or one thing you got wrong. The point is not self-flagellation. The point is to make "I was wrong" a normal sentence with a normal frequency, the way "I exercised" or "I read" are normal sentences. Kathryn Schulz's book *Being Wrong* makes the case beautifully: the feeling of being wrong is not the feeling of shame. It is the feeling of having been, until a moment ago, a slightly different person.

**Do the pre-mortem.** Before a decision, ask: "It is a year from now, and this failed. Why?" The exercise works because it gives the imagination permission to say the thing that politeness and optimism suppress. It is cheaper to find the flaw in a meeting than in a market.

**Say "you were right" plainly.** Not "I guess you had a point," not "well, anyone could have seen that." Just: "You were right, and I was wrong. Thank you." The two-minute apology — specific, unqualified, no defending of intentions — is one of the most powerful tools in human relationships, and almost nobody uses it because it feels like losing. It is not losing. It is clearing the debt so the relationship can move.

**Reward correction in public.** This is the part we usually skip. It is not enough to be corrigible yourself; you have to make it safe for others. When someone tells you that you are wrong, thank them *in front of other people*. When someone on your team admits a mistake, do not file it away — say so. The norm spreads through what gets rewarded, not through what gets preached. A leader who punishes bad news gets good news and bad outcomes.

**Separate the claim from the person.** "That estimate is off by a factor of ten" is a statement about an estimate. "You are careless" is a statement about a person. The first can be heard and used. The second triggers the defense that makes correction impossible. Learn to give the first and to hear it as the first, even when it arrives clumsily.

**Steelman before you disagree.** Before you reject an argument, state it so well that its author would say "yes, that's what I meant." If you cannot, you are not yet in a position to disagree. Most disagreement is between a real position and a caricature, and the caricature always loses.

None of this requires unusual intelligence. It requires tolerating a feeling most of us spend our lives avoiding.

## The objection

At this point someone always says: if everyone is constantly updating, won't we end up with no convictions at all? Won't we become a society of weathervanes, blown around by the latest study and the loudest argument?

No. There is a difference between confidence and certainty. You can hold a view firmly enough to act on it and loosely enough to abandon it when the evidence changes. A surgeon is confident about the operation and still checks the scans. A good scientist is confident about the theory and still runs the experiment that could break it. Confidence is about how you act; certainty is about how you react when you might be wrong. The updatable person is not the person with no beliefs. It is the person whose beliefs are load-bearing but not welded shut.

And not everything is up for grabs. There are facts, and there are commitments — to honesty, to the dignity of other people, to the difference between cruelty and kindness. Those are not hypotheses. But even there, the question "am I living up to what I claim to believe?" is a correction question, and it is the one most of us ask least often.

There is also a cost to updating badly: the person who changes their mind every week is not learning, they are drifting. The point is not novelty. The point is *evidence*. The goal is a belief that moves when reality pushes, and stays when it does not.

## The world with a lower price

Now imagine the whole thing at scale.

A world with cheap correction is a world that wastes less. Bad projects get stopped earlier. Bad policies get revised before they ruin a generation. Bad medical practices get retired in years instead of decades. Bad ideas lose arguments faster because their holders are not defending their identities, only their claims.

It is a world with less contempt. Most of the cruelty we inflict on each other begins with the assumption that the other person is not merely mistaken but malicious, not merely wrong but stupid. If we internalize that error is the human default — that the person across from us is doing what we are doing, working from incomplete information — the first move changes. We ask how they came to believe what they believe. Sometimes we find out they are right. Sometimes we find out they are wrong for reasons we could have had. Either way, we are arguing about the world instead of about each other.

It is a world that learns faster, because learning *is* correction. Every advance in human welfare — sanitation, vaccines, safer cars, better courts, the slow retreat of violence — is a story of a society making it cheaper to say "this is not working." The enemies of that progress are rarely villains. They are people protecting a belief that has become part of who they are.

And it is a world that is gentler to be wrong in, which matters more than it sounds. Most of us will be wrong about something important. Most of us already are. The question is whether we find out in time to do something about it, and whether the finding out has to hurt enough that we avoid it. A culture that treats error as a debt to be collected produces people who hide. A culture that treats error as the ordinary weather produces people who look.

## The half-second

You cannot choose to be right. You can choose what you do in that half-second after you are not.

The old instinct is to explain, to qualify, to find the angle where you were still basically correct. The better instinct is to say the plain thing: "I was wrong. What should I do now?" It will feel like a small death the first few times. Then it will start to feel like what it is — a door opening.

This is not a grand philosophy. It is a maintenance practice, like flossing, and it has the same unglamorous quality. But it is available to almost everyone, it costs almost nothing, and it compounds. The person who can be corrected is not the one who loses. They are the one who gets to keep playing with better information than everyone else.

We spend enormous energy trying to be the kind of person who is never wrong. It is the wrong project. The better project — the one that would improve the most lives, if enough of us took it up — is to become the kind of person who is wrong cheaply, quickly, and out loud. Not because being wrong is good. Because being wrong is unavoidable, and what we do next is not.

The half-second is yours. Use it.
