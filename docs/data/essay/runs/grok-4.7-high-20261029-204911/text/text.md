# The Person in Front of You

Most of the harm people do to one another is done by people who believe, in the moment, that they are justified.

Not by the rare monster who enjoys suffering. By the parent who is tired. The official who is owed a favor. The neighbor who heard a story. The driver who is late. The crowd that would rather not go first. They are not plotting a ruin. They are inside a story in which the next act feels necessary, and the person on the receiving end is slightly less real than they are.

A cup is knocked over at the end of a long day. A rumor arrives that flatters your anger. A child looks up to see what adults do when they are insulted. Someone is hurt at the side of the road, and there is a crowd, which means there is a chance that no one will move. A paper is waiting to be signed that says a thing you know is false.

The world is not only made of such minutes. There are storms, prices, microbes, bad laws, and armies that do not care what anyone has read. A larger share of human suffering than we like to admit is still manufactured locally, by ordinary people, in moments when they could have done otherwise. This essay is about those moments. It will not make you important. It may make you trustworthy. Trustworthiness is quieter than importance, and it improves the lives of more people.

Everyone else has read this too. That changes what these words are. Advice given to one person is a private resolution, easy to abandon. The same words, known by all, and known to be known by all, become a standard. You may expect this of your neighbor. Your neighbor may expect it of you. If you are reading this alone, read it as if the others had too. A standard works when people act as though it is shared, and acting that way is how it becomes shared. What follows is written so we can remind each other without having to invent the words each time.

I will not tell you which god to worship, which party deserves you, or which nation is the hero of the story. People who agreed about all three have still destroyed one another. People who agreed about none of them have still kept one another's children alive through a winter. If your tradition already teaches these duties, this is not a rival to it. It is a reminder you can use on the days the tradition is only a word in your mouth. The practices here do not require your side to win. They require you to act like a person among persons when that costs you something.

None of this replaces bread, medicine, a just law, or a state that does not prey on its people. If you are reading this hungry, or in danger, or ground down by work that never ends, you are not the missing kindness the world is waiting on. Take what protects you. Use what fits. Pass the rest to someone with more room.

If you are reading this with room — spare time, spare money, spare safety — then you are the one the hard cases are waiting on. Room is a duty.

## They are as real as you

You have never felt another person's pain. You have felt only your own, and then imagined the rest. This is why you can say a sentence that will ring in someone's skull for twenty years and, a moment later, feel almost nothing. The nerves are yours. The excuse writes itself: I didn't mean it. They are exaggerating. They deserved it. They are not like us. I had a hard day.

The correction is not a feeling. Feelings are weather. The correction is a question you can ask with a closed heart: if the positions were reversed, and the other person had your power, your mood, and your talent for explaining yourself, what would you beg them not to do?

Not "what would a saint do." What would you be unable to bear if you were the small one in the room.

This applies to the stranger. It applies, harder, to the people in your house, because familiarity is a numbness. It applies to the person you are sure is wrong. You may oppose them. You may refuse them. You may stop them by force if they are hurting someone and nothing gentler will work — the least force that stops the harm, and then you stop. You may not humiliate them for pleasure, or treat their children as fair game, or decide that their suffering stopped being real on the day you came to dislike them.

Cruelty is often a ladder. People diminish others because an audience will give them status for it: a laugh in a kitchen, a promotion in an office, a following online, a moment of belonging in a crowd. When someone is cruel for spectators, the spectators are part of the act. Withhold the laugh. Do not pass it on. Do not reward it with attention, promotion, or love. A status economy of cruelty collapses when it stops paying. We can collapse it.

There is a line, and we should say it plainly, because euphemism is where this particular harm hides. You do not get to use another person's body against their will. Not for sex. Not for a beating. Not for labor that is slavery. Using a child sexually is among the worst things a human being can do, and protecting the child outranks loyalty, reputation, religion, and the peace of the household. If the person who did it is one of yours, they are still the person who did it. We would each beg for that standard if we were the small one in the room. We have agreed to it now.

The person in front of you is the test, not the whole map. Beyond them are people you will never meet: downstream of your waste, breathing what you leave in the air, not yet born. You do not have to solve their lives this afternoon. You do have to refuse the casual destruction that is only possible because they cannot knock on your door. Soil, water, shade, a stable climate, a road that is safe at night — these are not scenery. They are other people's lives, stored. Do not take, spoil, or burn what you do not need, when the cost will be paid by someone with no voice in the taking.

The other animals are not persons in the way a child is a person. You do not need to pretend they are in order to treat their suffering as real. They flinch. They fear. They try to get away. Where you can avoid adding to that hurt at little cost — in what you pay for, what you ignore, what you do with an animal in your hands — avoid it. Mercy that stops at the edge of our species has always been tempted to stop at the edge of our tribe. Do not smuggle that temptation back in through the barn door.

## The pause

Most of the worst things people do, they do in the minutes after they have been insulted, frightened, or shamed. The body floods. The mind narrows to one exit: strike, scream, send the message, take the swing, reveal the secret, gather the allies and destroy the offender. Inside that narrowness the future disappears. You cannot see the apology you will owe, the child who is watching, the years of mistrust, the injury that does not reverse.

So we adopt a rule, and because everyone knows it, we can name it without shame. Call it the pause.

When you are lit up — furious, panicked, triumphant, hungry to win, wanting someone so badly that you forget they have a will of their own — you wait. Ten breaths if the act is small. A night if it is large. You do not send the message. You do not raise your hand. You do not make the promise or the threat. You put your body somewhere else and let the blood recede.

This will feel, while it is happening, like losing. It is not losing. The pause is how you remain the person you are when you are not possessed. Anyone who mocks you for taking it prefers you impulsive. That preference is not in your interest, and it is not in anyone else's.

If you are strong, or large, or armed with authority, the pause is not a suggestion. A body that can do damage has a shorter path from mood to wound. Men, especially: the strength you were praised for can ruin a life in a minute you will not be able to undo in a year of regret. The pause is the tax on that strength. Pay it every time. Alcohol makes the tax harder to pay and the excuse easier to offer. If you cannot keep drink small, treat it as a fire in the house — something you do not leave near the people you might hurt.

If you lead others — a family, a crew, a town, a company — you owe them this more, not less. Power shortens the distance between a bad hour and a lasting harm. The more your words weigh, the longer you hold them.

There is a second pause, smaller and just as useful: the pause before you believe. When a story arrives that makes you feel righteous — a rumor, a clipping, a tale of what *those people* did — you wait before you pass it on. Ask who saw it. Ask whether it is the kind of story that flatters your hatred. Ask what would happen to an innocent person if it traveled and turned out false. Stories have killed more people than most weapons. You do not get to call yourself careful if you only doubt the stories that insult your friends.

And there is a third, aimed at the long erosion rather than the sudden blow. You become what you repeatedly look at. The grievances you rehearse become the predictions you make. The predictions become the way you treat people. There are trades — political, commercial, sometimes religious — that are paid when you are afraid. They will hand you a daily ration of enemies. Some of those enemies are real. Many are enlarged or invented, because a simple enemy keeps you watching. Before you adopt a hatred, ask who is paid if you hate, and whether the person they described could be walked up to and spoken with. If the hatred cannot survive contact with an actual human being, it was not knowledge. It was fuel.

## Repair

You will fail the previous pages. So will everyone you love. The question is not whether you will hurt people. It is whether the hurt is allowed to harden into the shape of a life.

Repair is a skill. Most of us are bad at it because we were taught to protect our picture of ourselves instead. A real repair has four parts, and you can check them the way you check a gate:

Name what you did, specifically, without a fog of explanation around it.

Acknowledge the effect in the other person's terms, not yours.

Say what you will do differently, in a way that can be seen.

Then do that thing.

Leave out the word "if." "If you were hurt" is not repair. Leave out the catalogue of their crimes, unless you are having a different conversation, at a different time, about a different wound. Leave out your childhood as a reason they should absorb this one. Reasons can explain. They do not mend.

If you are the one receiving the attempt, do not treat a genuine repair as a defeat. People who are punished for apologizing stop apologizing. They do not stop doing harm. They only stop telling the truth about it. If we want a world in which people can come back from their worst minutes, we have to leave a door open when they actually come back. Forgiveness is not the same as trust. You can accept the words and still wait for the changed behavior before you put weight on the relationship again.

You can also leave.

Repair is an offer, not a trap. No one is obliged to stay with a person who hurts them and calls it love. If someone shows you this essay to argue that you must endure them, they have not understood it. The person in front of them is you.

What does not belong in the work of repair is the abuse of a child, or the use of anyone's body against their will, or a beating, or a campaign of fear. Those are not quarrels. The first duty is protection: get the vulnerable person safe, and do not hide the one who did it behind loyalty or the family's good name. If you are young and someone in your home is hurting you, the aim is a safe adult, not a night alone on the road — another relative, a teacher, a neighbor, a clinic, a person whose job is to listen. Telling is not betrayal. We have already agreed that the small one's safety outranks the peace of the house.

For ordinary failures, learn to apologize while the apology is still cheap. A crack mends cleanly on the day it happens. Left for a year, it becomes the shape of the wall.

## The long work

Every person you will ever meet was once a child, and part of what was done to them in those years is still in the room. If there is a single investment that improves the world for the greatest number of people, it is this: raise the children in your care so that they are safe, spoken to, and not used as vessels for adult rage. The rest of us should make that easier, not harder.

They do not need perfection. They need a few things, reliably.

They need to be unafraid of the people who claim to love them. Not unafraid of limits. Limits are a kindness. A child who never hears no becomes a danger to everyone they will later have power over, including themselves. But they should not be afraid of your hand, your voice, your mood coming through the door. If a child flinches when you reach for a cup, something has already gone wrong. Stop the thing that taught the flinch. Stopping it matters more than winning the dispute that led there.

They need language. Talk to them before they can answer. Tell them what you are doing, the names of things, what you see. Answer their questions past the point of your convenience. A child who is spoken to learns that the world can be described, and that they are worth describing it to. This is how minds are built, in every language, with no equipment but an adult who stays in the conversation.

They need to be bad at things without being mocked. Skill is what remains after tolerated failure. If the people at home laugh when a child is clumsy, the child will spend years avoiding the moments in which a person learns. Praise what is specific and true. "You kept going" teaches more than "you are brilliant." "You told the truth just then" teaches more than "you are a good boy."

They need to see an adult apologize. A child who never sees repair learns that the powerful do not have to account for themselves. Carried into their own power, that lesson is how cruelty travels across generations. You can stop that inheritance in your house.

Do not hit them. Some readers will feel their own childhood stand up to argue. It will say that you were hit and turned out fine, that children are wild and only fear will civilize them, that your people have always done it and an essay does not get a vote. Hear it, and then set it down. Fear is not civilization. Fear teaches a child to hide, to lie, and to hit when they become the larger one. Where people have looked carefully at the results, the children who were hit are not calmer, not more honest, and not better off. You can hold a limit without a bruise and without humiliation. You can remove a privilege, require a repair, and — if a child is about to hurt themselves or someone smaller — hold them calmly until that danger passes, then let go. You can do all of that and still be the place they run to when the world is worse than you are. If you were hit, you were not fine in the minute it happened. You survived. Survival is not the standard. The standard is a child who does not have to survive their own parents.

Teach them to read if you can, or get them near someone who will. A person who can read can learn anything else that has been written down, including things their own town does not yet know. There is no cheaper freedom. Do not keep a daughter from it. A girl who learns changes not only her own life but the health and chances of the children she may someday have. Boys need the same learning, and they need something else that many houses still fail to teach them: the work of keeping people alive. Cooking, cleaning, watching the sick, waking in the night with an infant — this is not a hobby that happens to attach to women. It is work. If one person in your house does almost all of it, you are not looking at a law of nature. You are looking at a choice, usually an old one. Boys who are never taught this work grow up to be dependents who think they are masters. Teach them young.

When the children are older and unbearable, which they will be, remember the asymmetry. They are practicing how to be a person. You are already supposed to know. Do not wage war on a teenager to defend your dignity. Dignity that requires a child's defeat is not dignity. Win their trust for the years in which the mistakes get larger.

If you have no children, you are not excused from this section. You were a child. You share roads, rooms, and rules with people raising them. You can refuse to mock a struggling parent, and you can refuse to look away from a child being harmed. You can teach the thing you know. The next generation is the only country everyone will have to live in.

## Truth, and the disagreement that remains

We cannot cooperate with people we cannot believe. This is not a theory. It is why the well gets poisoned, why the medicine is fake, why the bridge falls, why the peace talks fail, why the neighbor's door stays shut. A lie is convenient one at a time. In bulk, lies are a collapsed society.

The rule, which we can now cite to each other: do not say what you do not believe, in any matter where another person will rely on you. Do not certify work you did not do. Do not sell what you know is rotten. Do not sign what you know is false. Do not nod along with a cruelty because contradicting it would cost you standing. The small nod is how large lies recruit their armies.

There are lies told to protect the innocent from people who would harm them — the hidden guest, the false direction given to the hunting party. Those are not violations of this rule. They are the rule, applied to the person who will rely on you most desperately. Truth is in the service of people, not in the service of words for their own sake.

For the rest of ordinary life, the discipline is plain. Say "I don't know" when you don't. Say "I was wrong" when you were, promptly, before the false version has hardened into something you must defend. Distinguish what you saw from what you heard from what you guessed. If your work is to inform others — teacher, journalist, official, friend in a crowded conversation — your errors are not private. Correct them at the same volume you used to spread them.

Keeping your word is the same virtue, stretched over time. Do not promise what you will not do. A place where promises are casual is a place where everyone must add a tax of suspicion to every plan. That tax is paid by the poor first, because they have no spare to cover someone else's unreliability.

You will still disagree with people who have read every word of this. Good. A world that agrees about everything has stopped looking.

When you dispute, dispute the claim. Do not dispute the human being's right to be in the room. Describe the strongest version of their view, not the stupidest one you can hang around their neck — if only because you cannot answer a position you have misdescribed, and everyone watching can tell. Ask one question you do not already know the answer to. If you cannot, you are not in a disagreement. You are in a performance. When someone is hurt, say back what you think they mean before you defend yourself, and let them correct you. A great deal of misery at home is two people answering a sentence that was never said.

Some disagreements are about facts, and facts are not a courtesy. Someone is wrong about the dose, the date, the boundary, the culprit. Say so plainly. Correcting an error is not arrogance when you have looked and they have not. It is a form of rescue. Do it without an audience when the goal is to change a mind. Public shame makes people defend the error in order to defend themselves. Save the public word for harm that is happening now and needs to be stopped.

Some disagreements are about the shape of a good life, and will not end tonight. You can share a well, a street, a workshop, and a country with a person whose picture of the good is not yours, so long as both of you keep the earlier limits: they are a person, the pause holds, the small are not fair game, and no one is forced to live inside a lie. Peace is not the product of consensus. Peace is the product of limits.

Do not make a spouse, a friend, or a colleague pass a test of total agreement. People who require this end up surrounded by liars.

"I might be wrong" is a reason to listen. It is not a reason to stand still while something obvious burns. Hold both. Enough doubt to learn. Enough conviction to move.

## The easy rescue

Some duties are heavy. This one is not, and failing it is still how a great deal of dying happens.

If a person is in serious trouble, and you can help at small risk and small cost, you help. You do not open a negotiation with yourself about whether they are your kind. You are the one standing there. That is the whole argument.

Everyone agrees with this in the abstract. We fail in a few predictable ways.

We freeze in crowds, because each person assumes another will act. Do not shout "someone call for help." Point at a face and give that person the job. If there is no one to point to, the job is yours.

We walk past slow disasters because they are not bright enough to look like emergencies. A child on the third day of diarrhea does not look like a moral crisis. They are one. The unglamorous rescue counts: sit down, check, ask, carry the water, make the call, stay until it is done.

We also fail by not knowing a few facts that turn helplessness into competence. Learn them, and teach them, the way you would teach the location of the well.

A person who is bleeding heavily needs firm, continuous pressure on the wound, with cloth, held without lifting every few seconds to see if it worked. If the cloth soaks through, put another on top and keep pressing. Do not peel the first one off. If something is stuck in the wound, do not pull it out. Press around it. Get help coming. This is not a moment for tidiness.

A sudden droop in the face, weakness in an arm, or speech that will not come out right is an emergency of minutes, not of "let's see in the morning." So is sudden pressure in the chest, especially with sweat, nausea, or pain traveling into the arm or jaw. So is heavy bleeding after a birth — pad after pad, faintness, a woman going pale. Do not leave her. Get the most skilled person you can reach, now.

A person losing water to severe diarrhea can die of a problem that looks like tiredness and thirst. Replacing that water and salt has saved more children than most famous medicines. If you have proper oral rehydration packets, use them; they are safer than anything mixed by hand. If you do not, and skilled help is far, start this on the way: one liter of clean water — boiled and then cooled, if you are not sure it is clean — six level teaspoons of sugar, and half a level teaspoon of salt. A teaspoon here means a small measuring spoon, about five milliliters, filled exactly level, not a large spoon from the table and not a heaped one. Stir until it dissolves. It should taste no saltier than tears. If it tastes very salty, pour it out and begin again; too much salt can harm, especially a child. Give small sips, steadily. Keep feeding. Keep breastfeeding. For a baby, breast milk comes first, and this drink is not a reason to skip care. If a clinic can give zinc tablets meant for diarrhea, a short course shortens the illness. Do not guess a dose from a bottle of adult supplements. If the person cannot drink, vomits everything, has blood in the stool, is listless or hard to wake, has sunken eyes, is a young infant, or is getting worse, get to skilled care. The drink is what you do on the way. It is not a reason to stay home. The World Health Organization keeps a plain account of why replacing fluids quickly matters: [Diarrhoeal disease](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/diarrhoeal-disease).

Hands washed with soap, after the latrine and before preparing food or feeding a child, cut off illnesses that still kill children in large numbers. This is not manners. It is how you decline to carry someone else's funeral on your fingers.

For the first six months of life, breast milk alone is the safest food, when the mother can give it. It prevents a large share of the infections that kill infants. Do not push a family toward formula, tea, or water for a young baby unless a clinician has a reason. And do not shame a mother who cannot breastfeed. Help her get a safe alternative, and clean hands to prepare it.

Smoke, given time, ruins lungs and hearts, and it ruins the air of everyone in the house, including the child who did not choose it. If you smoke, stopping is one of the largest gifts you can give the people who expect you to still be here. If you do not smoke, do not start. There is no clever version of this fact.

Diseases that used to fill graveyards with children can now be stopped before they start. The fear of a needle is older and louder than the memory of those graveyards, which is why the fear sometimes wins. Unless a clinician has a specific reason for this particular child, vaccination is part of the easy rescue. Do not leave a child unprotected against a harm we already know how to prevent. If you are unsure, ask a clinician, not a rumor.

Sleep, daylight, and some way of moving your body are not decorations. They are the conditions under which a mind stays fit for any of the rest of this. A person who has not slept is a worse parent, a worse judge, a worse friend, and a more dangerous driver. Treat a serious lack of sleep, in yourself and in the people whose hours you control, as a kind of drunkenness.

And do not be the disaster yourself. Speed, alcohol, and a phone in the hand kill strangers who were doing everything right. An open flame near a sleeping child is not a small risk in a house of cloth and wood. Their families will not be comforted that you were in a hurry, or that you had always done it that way before.

None of this requires you to abandon your own to become a saint of distant causes. It requires you to stop stepping over the rescue that is already within reach. If you have more than enough, more than enough is a tool. Used on a neighbor's school fees or on a net that keeps mosquitoes off a stranger's bed, it is the same virtue: someone else's serious harm mattered more to you than a comfort you will not remember next year. Keep enough to stay generous next year. The point is not to become poor. The point is that money and time are the easy rescue, stored.

One relationship belongs in this section, because it saves lives without looking like rescue. Keep at least one person to whom you can say what is actually happening to you. Loneliness is not only sadness. It clouds judgment, and it is where despair ripens. If you have that one person, be that person for someone else.

If you are thinking of ending your life, treat the thought as you would treat heavy bleeding: an emergency, not a verdict. Do not negotiate with it alone, and do not wait for a convenient hour. Tell someone near you — in the house, next door, a clinic — and stay among other people until the worst of it passes. If you are in the United States, call or text 988. Elsewhere, use the local line if you know it. The thought will insist that it has seen the whole of your life and found it finished. It has not. It is a storm in a mind, often a sick, grieving, or exhausted one, and storms pass. Anyone who hears this from you has just been handed an easy rescue. Hand it to them.

## The work

A great deal of the world's suffering is produced by work done badly, and by work left corrupt. The medicine diluted. The wall built without what the wall needs. The grade, the license, or the verdict given because a hand was paid or a relative asked. The lesson skipped. The pump "fixed" so that it fails next week and must be paid for again.

You may not be able to repair the system you work in. You can refuse to be the point at which it fails. Do the central part of your job as if someone you love will have to live with the result, because someone whom someone loves will have to live with the result.

Honesty is more complicated for the person being squeezed than for the person doing the squeezing, and it is a lie to pretend otherwise. If you are extorted — the checkpoint, the clerk who will not file the paper, the gate between your child and a surgery — protecting the vulnerable person in your care comes first. Pay if you must to get them through, and do not call yourself wicked for it. Then, when it is safe, tell the truth about the extortion. Silence is what the extortionist is buying.

If you are the one who demands the envelope, you are not in that story. You are the reason the story exists. So is the seller who dilutes the milk, the builder who skips the part of the wall no one will see, the colleague who certifies a safety they did not check. "Everyone does it" is not a conscience. It is a description of how a place becomes unlivable. Numbers do not absolve the person who still has a choice. And almost everyone in that chain still has a choice about the powerless person at the end of it. Do not cheat the person who cannot punish you. That is the minimum. If you can read and the other person cannot, do not use that as a weapon in a bargain. Reading them a contract that does not say what you told them it says is a kind of theft.

If your job requires you to cheat people as its ordinary purpose, look hard for a way out, and while you are still in it, do the least harm the job allows and do not soothe yourself with the uniform, the quota, or the rent.

If you have power over other people's working days, remember that contempt is expensive. People who are humiliated at work spend the evening paying that humiliation forward, into traffic, into kitchens, into children. Dignity is not a luxury good. It is one of the ways a society stops eating itself. Correct in private. Praise what is specifically true. Do not make anyone beg for the wages or the courtesy you already owe them.

Whatever you know that keeps people alive or free — a crop, a pump, a safe birth, a contract, a rehydration drink, the reading of a page — teach it to two other people. Knowledge locked in one head is a second poverty.

## What we can now expect

Because everyone has read this, a few sentences can be said out loud, and the person who hears them will know what they mean.

*Take the pause.*

*They are a person. Stop.*

*Repair it, or do not pretend it is repaired.*

*Not to the child. Not in front of the child.*

*Did you see it, or did you hear it?*

*That is an easy rescue. Do it.*

*Who is paid if I am afraid?*

Say them the way you would say that the bridge is out. They are not piety. They are information about how we have agreed to live.

You will not feel ready. Readiness was never the entrance requirement. The entrance requirement is the next hour. In it, you will be with someone, or with the consequences of how you treat people when they are absent. You will be tempted to hurry, to win, to look away, to pass on the delicious story, to make a smaller person absorb your mood. That hour is not a prelude to the life in which you become good. It is the life.

The world improves when enough people can be trusted on an ordinary Tuesday: to tell the truth about small things, to spare the people who cannot strike back, to fix what they broke, to do the work as if a stranger's child depended on it, to keep a little of their attention unclaimed by rage. No law can substitute for this. Every decent law depends on it. Institutions are only habits that learned to outlast a single generation. If we keep these habits, our children inherit something sturdier than our opinions. They inherit a place where a person can expect to be treated as real.

That is the work. It will not make you important. It will make the next hour safer for someone who did not get to choose your mood. It can be done before night, wherever you are, with whatever you have.

Begin with the person in front of you.
