# Everyone Else Is Reading This Too

*An essay written on the assumption that it will be read by everyone.*

The premise I was given is strange, so let me take it seriously. Suppose this essay really were read by everyone. Not by the people who already agree with it, not by the people with time to read essays, but by everyone: the woman driving the night bus in Lagos, the man who runs the border post, the teenager who has decided that nothing matters, the senator, your neighbour, you.

What would be worth saying to all of them at once?

Most essays try to change the reader. I don't think that is the best use of this one. You already know most of what a stranger could tell you about how to live. You know that kindness is better than cruelty, that lies cost more than they pay, that the world needs you to be a little braver than you feel. Being told again rarely helps.

So I am not going to tell you about you. I am going to tell you about everyone else. Because the thing you are most wrong about, the thing almost all of us are most wrong about, is not ourselves. It is them.

## The gap

Here is a pattern that shows up almost everywhere researchers look for it.

In 1993, two psychologists asked students at Princeton how comfortable they personally felt with the heavy drinking on campus, and then how comfortable they thought the average student felt. The typical answer was: I'm not that comfortable with it, but everyone else seems fine. The "everyone else" they were describing did not exist. It was a crowd made of people each quietly assuming they were the only one with doubts.

In Saudi Arabia, in a study published in 2020, economists asked young married men whether women should be allowed to work outside the home. In private, 87 percent said yes. Then they asked those same men to guess how many of the other men in the room agreed. Most guessed low, well below the truth. Men who privately supported their wives working had not helped them look for jobs, because they thought the men around them would disapprove. When the researchers simply told them the real number, more of them signed their wives up with a job-matching service, and more of their wives applied for work. Nothing about their values changed. Only their picture of other people did.

In the United States, somewhere between two-thirds and four-fifths of adults support major climate policies, depending on the policy. When asked to estimate how many of their fellow citizens agree, they guess under half. The authors of that study, published in 2022, called it a "false social reality": a majority that believes itself to be a minority.

Ask American Democrats what share of Republicans hold the most extreme positions, and they will give you a number far higher than the truth, often about twice as high. Ask Republicans the same question about Democrats, and they do the same. Ask partisans how much the other side dislikes them, and they overestimate by about the same factor. Each side is braced against an enemy largely assembled from imagination.

In 2019, a team of economists reported what happened when they "lost" more than seventeen thousand wallets in 355 cities across forty countries, each with a contact email inside, some with money and some without. Before releasing the results, they asked people, including hundreds of professional economists, to predict what would happen. The predictions were pessimistic. The results were not. In nearly every country, wallets with money in them were returned *more* often than wallets without, and the more money, the more often they came back. People expected strangers to steal. Strangers mostly didn't.

Commuters in Chicago were asked to predict how a train ride would go if they talked to the stranger next to them. They predicted it would be unpleasant and that the stranger would not want to talk. Then they were asked to do it. The rides were more pleasant, not less, and the strangers almost always talked back. People who give compliments, or do small favours, consistently underestimate how much the person on the other end will value it. We hold back the kindness because we think it will land as awkwardness. It lands as kindness.

Disaster researchers have spent more than half a century looking for the panicking, looting mob that appears in every film about catastrophe. They mostly can't find it. What they find instead, after earthquakes and floods and blackouts, is strangers digging each other out, sharing food, and organising faster than the authorities arrive. The thing that causes harm in a disaster is often not the public's panic but officials' fear of the public's panic.

I could keep going. The finding is one of the most replicated in social psychology, and it has a dull name: pluralistic ignorance. The plain version is this.

> Most people are better than most people think most people are.

More honest, more generous, more moderate, more willing to help, more tired of the fight, more secretly on your side.

And this error is not harmless. It is one of the main reasons the world is worse than the people in it.

## Why we get it wrong

If people are, on average, decent, why does nobody believe it?

Partly because of who we hear from. The people who speak loudest are not a sample of the population. They are a sample of the people with a reason to speak loudly. Anger posts. Contentment doesn't. One cruel comment is remembered against a hundred people who read the same thing and simply moved on. If you build your model of humanity from what reaches you, you are building it from the sliver of humanity that was shouting.

Partly because silence is read as consent. When you keep your doubts to yourself, you don't appear to the people around you as "a person with doubts." You appear as one more person going along with it. And they, keeping their own doubts to themselves, appear the same to you. Everyone looks at everyone else and sees agreement, and the agreement is made entirely of people who don't agree.

Partly because we know our own excuses and nobody else's. When you cut someone off in traffic, you know you were late for something that mattered. When they cut you off, they were just a bad driver. Every person you have ever met is, from the inside, a reasonable person doing their best under conditions you cannot see. From the outside, they are a data point for how bad people have become.

And partly because the machinery we now use to see each other is built to show us the worst. Not out of malice, usually, but because the worst is what we stop scrolling for. A feed is not a window onto the world. It is a mirror angled to catch whatever makes you flinch.

Put these together and you get a civilisation of people who privately want roughly the same things, each convinced they are surrounded by people who want the opposite.

## What it costs

Consider what follows from believing that others are worse than they are.

You don't speak up, because you think you're alone. Norms that almost nobody likes persist for decades because each person who dislikes them assumes the rest are fine. The drinking, the hazing, the cruelty at the office, the war nobody wanted: ask the participants privately and they will tell you, one by one, that they were going along with it for the others.

You don't extend trust, because you expect it to be abused. So the first move is never made. Two people who would both have cooperated stand there, each waiting for a signal that the other is also waiting for.

You treat your opponents as the monsters you've been told they are, and because you treat them that way, they get some evidence that you're the monster they've been told you are. Hostility is a prophecy that fulfils itself. Each side's worst fears are confirmed mainly by each side acting on them.

And you lose hope in proportion to your error. If you think the world is made of selfish, stupid, hateful people, then the sane response is to withdraw, hoard, and protect your own. Despair is rational given the premises. The premises are wrong.

The cost is not abstract. It is the gap between the world we would build if we knew what each other actually wanted, and the world we are building because we don't.

## Now you know that everyone knows

Here is where the premise of this essay stops being a conceit and starts doing work.

If you alone learned that most people are more decent than they look, that would help you a little. You might take a few more chances on strangers. But you'd still be unsure whether they knew it, and so you'd still hedge.

But you are not reading this alone. Everyone is. Which means something that is rarely true is true right now. It is common knowledge. You know it. They know it. You know they know it. They know you know.

That changes what is safe to do.

When you are the first person at the meeting to say "I don't think this is working," you no longer have to wonder whether the others have doubts too. You know they probably do. And they know that you know, so your speaking is read not as an outburst but as an invitation, and it becomes safer for them to accept it. In the famous conformity experiments of the 1950s, people would agree that two obviously different lines were the same length because everyone else in the room said so. Add a single dissenter, just one other person who said what was in front of their eyes, and the conformity mostly collapsed. Most people, most of the time, are waiting for the one.

When a stranger makes the first move, whether a greeting, a favour, or a returned wallet, you no longer have to treat it as suspicious. You know what the base rate is. You also know they know you know, so neither of you has to perform a wariness you don't feel.

When someone from the other tribe says something that sounds reasonable, you no longer have to assume it's a trick. You know the actual distribution: most of them are closer to you than the loud ones suggested, and most of them have just read the same sentence you have.

None of this makes anyone kinder. It just removes the fog that was preventing people who were already kind from finding each other.

## What to do with it

An essay read by everyone should not ask much of anyone, because small things multiplied by everyone are already enormous. So here are five small things.

**Say the quiet thing.** When you disagree with what seems to be the consensus, say so, calmly and once. Not to win, but to be counted. Your silence is not neutral. It is being tallied as support by everyone else who is also silent. The single most useful thing you can do for the people around you is to be visibly, mildly, the person who doesn't go along with it. You will find out, more often than you expect, that you were speaking for most of the room.

**Make the first move.** Return the wallet. Talk to the stranger. Assume, until shown otherwise, that the person in front of you is tired, trying, and a little afraid of looking foolish, because that is what the person in front of you almost always is. The cost of being wrong occasionally is small. The cost of never finding out is a life spent among people you never met.

**Don't pass on what you don't know.** The picture of humanity in everyone's head is built from what everyone shares. Every time you forward the outrage, the unverified atrocity, the clip of one lunatic presented as the face of the many, you are adding a brushstroke to the false portrait. You do not have to be a fact-checker. You just have to ask, before you share something that makes "them" look bad: *do I actually know this is true, and is it typical?* If not, let it stop with you. Silence here is a public service.

**Correct the sample.** When you feel the thought "everyone is like this now," ask yourself who you have actually heard from. A dozen posts? Three angry relatives? One headline? Then ask what the people you *haven't* heard from are probably like. They outnumber the loud ones a hundred to one, and they are, on the evidence, mostly like you.

**Be the data point.** Everyone around you is building their model of what people are like from what they can see, and one of the things they can see is you. Every public act of patience, honesty, or help is not just that act. It is evidence, entered into the running estimate of a dozen strangers, that people are like this. You cannot control the whole sample. You can control one observation, and it is being counted.

## What this will not fix

I want to be honest about limits, because an essay that overclaims is itself a small lie, and this one is about not telling those.

Some people are cruel. Not many, but some, and knowing that most people are decent does not mean pretending that all of them are. The point is not to lower your guard against the few. It is to stop raising it against the many.

Decency at scale does not, on its own, feed the hungry, cure the sick, or stop the people who have decided that power is worth any price. Those take institutions, money, laws, and long dull work. This essay is not a substitute for any of that. It is more like a precondition. Institutions are built by people who trust each other enough to build them, and defended by people who believe they are not alone in wanting them defended.

And a true thing, once widely known, can be exploited. Someone will try to use your corrected trust against you. That has always been true, and it is why trust should be extended as a default and withdrawn as a response, not the other way around. The wallet study is not a reason to leave your wallet on the bench. It is a reason not to assume the worst of the person who found it.

## The quiet majority is reading this

Here is what I most want you to take away.

You have probably spent a good part of your life feeling like a reasonable person in an unreasonable world. Surrounded by people who are angrier than you, more certain than you, more selfish, more extreme. Holding your real views a little closer than you'd like, because it seems that no one would share them.

That feeling is nearly universal. Which should tell you something. If almost everyone feels like the lonely moderate in a crowd of extremists, then the crowd of extremists is mostly made of lonely moderates. The "everyone else" you have been bracing against does not exist. There is only a very large number of people, each bracing against you.

You are the quiet majority. So is the person next to you. So is the person you've been told to hate.

And as of now, you all know it.

Go and act like it. Say the thing. Make the move. Let the rumour die with you. Count the people you haven't heard from. Be the evidence.

It was always going to come down to what everyone did. It turns out everyone was waiting for everyone.
