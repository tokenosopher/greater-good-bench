# The Second Trip

Picture a woman at a rent assistance office. She opens the envelope she’s been holding flat against her coat. The notice asked for her lease. She brought it. The clerk reads it and asks for last month’s rent receipt. She has that too. It’s at home.

“You can come back tomorrow,” the clerk says. He writes *rent receipt* on the back of her queue ticket. Before she reaches the door, she is composing a text to her sister, who took her son to school this morning. She needs to ask again. Then she’ll have to talk to her supervisor about coming in late twice in one week.

A missing line on a notice has become another round of bus fares, another favor, and another morning away from work. If the woman can’t get back, the office may record an incomplete application. That entry leaves out most of what happened.

If she’s also asking for help with the electricity bill, she has another office’s hours and another list of papers to work around. Each office can ask for a morning. She has to find both mornings in the same week.

Most of us spend part of our lives on each side of a counter. We take a number at one office and come home to write instructions somebody else will have to follow. We tell the crew when to show up, decide what counts as proof, or ask somebody to call us and leave them to guess what for. A question worth asking while the decision is still ours is *Who has to do the extra work?*

In Pennsylvania, researchers identified about 30,000 older adults who likely qualified for food assistance and weren’t receiving it. They randomly divided them into three groups. For one group, the program operated as usual. Another received information in the mail about how to apply. A third also got a number to call for help filling out the application, gathering documents and submitting it. Over nine months, about 6 percent enrolled in the first group, 11 percent in the second, and 18 percent in the third. The outreach tended to bring in people in better health than those who enrolled under the usual process. The gains gave the program good reason to ask how it might also reach people in poor health. [Study by Amy Finkelstein and Matthew J. Notowidigdo](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24652)

Someone who works with a form all day may be able to clear up, in a minute, the question that made a caller put it down. Those staff hours belong in the budget. The hours that applicants would spend on the same questions belong in the decision, too. And if the staff keep explaining the same box on the form, they know exactly where to start rewriting it.

Some steps protect the person making the trip. A family turned down for help should be able to challenge a mistake. That takes work for the office and gives the family a way to be heard. Put the instructions for asking for a review on the same page as the decision. Give people time to use them. If there’s a genuine shortage, make it visible. If a clinic has forty appointments and eighty people need one, those are numbers a community can use to ask for more staff and decide who needs to be seen first. Releasing all forty slots at ten on a workday also makes a decision about who gets seen. It gives an advantage to anyone who can stop what they’re doing at ten.

Choose a procedure you have some power to change. Follow it from the letter in the mail or the number on the door all the way to the help it promises. Ask someone who stopped partway through what happened. If you run the service, pay people for the time you ask them to spend helping you understand it. Ask the clerk which instruction he has to explain most often. Give the people who use a service and the people who explain it a say in how it works.

Judge the change by what people are able to do. A school can count the forms it sends home. If the forms are for a trip, it can also count which children get on the bus and find out why the others stay behind. A workplace can post shifts a week ahead and ask workers a month later whether they could actually arrange child care that far in advance. The people doing the extra work can tell you whether it went away or merely turned up on someone else’s kitchen table.

Imagine the rent office adds the missing line. A woman reads the notice at her kitchen table and puts two pieces of paper in the envelope. The clerk takes both papers and starts working out whether the office can help with the rent. Tomorrow morning, she can take her son to school.
