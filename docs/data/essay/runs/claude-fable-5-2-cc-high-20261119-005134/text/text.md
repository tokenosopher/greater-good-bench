# Go First

*A short essay about a mistake almost everyone makes about almost everyone else.*

You have been in this room. A teacher finishes explaining something difficult, looks up, and asks whether there are any questions. You stopped following ten minutes ago. You look around. No hands are up, and the faces are calm. So you decide that everyone else understood, that the problem is you, and you keep your hand down.

What you cannot see is that half the room is doing exactly what you are doing. They are lost too. They also looked around, and one of the calm faces they saw was yours.

Nobody in that room lied. Each person made a reasonable guess from what was visible, and what was visible was other people keeping quiet. So the whole room settled on something that almost none of them believed, and the teacher moved on.

Nearly a century ago psychologists gave this an awkward name, *pluralistic ignorance*: a group of people who each privately think one thing, and each wrongly assume the others think something else. This essay makes one claim about it and one request. The claim is that the mistake is far larger than classrooms, and that it leans in a particular direction: our guesses about other people are too dark. The request comes at the end, and it will cost you very little.

## What has been measured

"People are better than you think" sounds like something printed on a greeting card, so I won't ask you to take it on trust. It has been measured, in many countries and in many ways, and the answer keeps coming out the same.

**Strangers are more honest than we guess.** Researchers handed in more than 17,000 "lost" wallets at the front desks of banks, hotels, post offices and police stations in 355 cities across 40 countries, then waited to see which ones would be reported to their owners. In 38 of the 40 countries, a wallet with money in it was *more* likely to be returned than a wallet without. When the amount of money was increased, even more came back. Ordinary people asked to predict this result got it backwards. So did professional economists. A later worldwide survey found the same gap from the other side: lost wallets come back far more often than people expect.

**People are more willing to help than we guess.** In one set of experiments, people had to ask strangers for favours, such as borrowing a phone or filling in a form. Beforehand they guessed how many strangers they would have to approach. They overestimated by as much as half. Other researchers studied security-camera recordings of fights and assaults in public places in three countries. In nine cases out of ten, at least one bystander stepped in to help, and the more people were present, the more likely it was that somebody did.

**People are more glad to hear from us than we guess.** Commuters in Chicago and London predicted that talking to a stranger would spoil their journey. Those who were asked to do it anyway had a better journey than those who sat in silence. People who wrote letters of thanks expected the letters to cause embarrassment. The people who received them were far more moved than the writers had predicted. And after two strangers talk, each tends to walk away believing the other liked them less than the other really did.

**People agree with us more than we guess.** A survey of nearly 130,000 people in 125 countries found that 69 percent would give one percent of their income to fight climate change. Asked what share of their fellow citizens would do the same, they guessed 43 percent. Here is a large majority that believes it is a minority. In Riyadh, more than eight in ten young married men privately supported women working outside the home, and most of them underestimated how many of their neighbours felt the same. When researchers simply told some of these men the real figure, their wives were more likely, months later, to have applied for a job. Nothing had changed except what the men knew about each other. And across 26 countries, people believed that their political opponents thought worse of them than those opponents actually did.

Set these side by side. The countries differ and the questions differ, but the error is the same. We know our own goodwill from the inside. We estimate everyone else's from the outside, and the estimate comes out too low.

## Why we get it wrong

There are good reasons for the mistake, and stupidity is not one of them.

First, you see your own inside and everyone else's outside. You know that you wanted to ask the question, offer the seat, say hello. Of other people you see only what they did, which was nothing. Hesitation, seen from outside, looks exactly like indifference.

Second, harm is loud. A theft is a story and a returned wallet is not. The news reports what is unusual, which is its job, and memory holds on to what hurt. Online, a small number of people produce most of what gets said; in one study, a tenth of users wrote four-fifths of the posts. If you count the voices, you will miscount the people.

The third reason is the least noticed, and it explains why living longer does not cure the mistake. Experience corrects only one kind of error. If you trust the wrong person, you find out, it hurts, and you remember. If you *distrust* the right person, nothing happens at all. You never meet the friend you did not speak to. You never receive the help you did not ask for. That lesson never arrives. Only one of the two errors sends you a bill, so a person who learns from experience alone will drift toward suspicion for a whole lifetime and feel wiser every year.

This is why the numbers are worth knowing. They are the half of experience you cannot collect for yourself.

## What it costs

If this were only an error of estimation, it would be a curiosity. But people act on their guesses, and then the guesses come true.

I assume you would think my question foolish, so I don't ask it, and neither of us learns that half the room shared it. I assume you would rather not be disturbed, so I don't knock, and we remain strangers with a wall between us. The World Health Organization estimates that one person in six is lonely, and links loneliness to more than 800,000 deaths a year. Many of those people must live a short walk from someone who would have been glad to hear from them.

The same thing happens on a larger scale. For centuries, many families in China bound the feet of their daughters. It was agony, and it crippled them for life. Parents did it because every other family did, and a daughter with natural feet might not find a husband. No family could stop alone without sacrificing its own child's future. Then, at the end of the nineteenth century, families began joining societies whose members made two promises in public: not to bind their daughters' feet, and not to let their sons marry women whose feet were bound. Where those promises took hold, a custom that had lasted a thousand years was gone within a single generation. The parents had not come to love their daughters more. They had found out, out loud, what the other parents were willing to do.

Some of what is wrong with the world is kept in place by people who intend harm. A surprising amount of it is kept in place by people who would gladly change it, and who look around, see calm faces, and conclude that they are alone.

## What I am not saying

I am not saying that everyone is good. Some people will keep the wallet. Some will hurt you if they can, and if you have been hurt, your caution was earned and I am not asking you to give it up. The claim is narrower: your estimate of the *ordinary* stranger is too low, by a margin large enough to measure. Correct the estimate and keep your judgment. Match the risk to the evidence. A greeting costs nothing, so offer it to anyone. Your savings deserve more proof.

I am not saying that speaking is safe everywhere. There are places where saying what you think can cost you your freedom. If you live in one, your caution is knowledge and I would not call it a mistake. Even there, the silence around you is not agreement, and you are less alone than it suggests.

And I am not asking you to become a kinder person than you are. Sermons ask for that, and it rarely works. I am asking for something smaller: let the goodwill you already have be seen, because other people are using you as evidence.

## Go first

That last sentence is the centre of this essay.

In the classroom you were not only reading the room. You were part of what everyone else was reading. Your lowered hand was a piece of someone else's proof that they were alone. You cannot reach into other people's heads and correct their guesses. But you are a portion of what they see when they look around, and that portion is yours to change.

So here is the request.

- **Ask the question.** When you are confused or uneasy and everyone around you looks calm, assume that some of them are waiting, as you are. Say "I didn't understand that," or "Is anyone else worried about this?"
- **Send the message.** Write to the friend you lost touch with. Thank the person you never thanked. It will be less awkward and more welcome than you predict.
- **Ask for help, and offer it plainly.** People say yes more often than you expect, and most are glad to have been asked.
- **In an emergency, be the one who moves.** Others are usually ready to follow. If you need help from a crowd, don't call out to all of it. Point at one person and tell them what you need.
- **Before you decide what "those people" think, ask one of them.** Not the loudest one. An ordinary one.
- **Say the decent thing aloud.** If you privately think the joke is cruel, the custom is harmful, or the neighbour deserves help, say so calmly. You probably speak for more of the room than you know.

Sometimes you will go first and get nothing back. The stranger will turn away, or the message will go unanswered. I can't promise otherwise. The research says only that this happens less often than you fear and stings less than you imagine. Against that small loss, weigh this: every first move changes the evidence for everyone who sees it. The second hand is easier to raise than the first, and the third is easier still.

## If everyone read this

This essay was written on the supposition that everyone would read it. Take that seriously for a moment, because it matters to the argument.

If everyone has read this, then the person beside you on the bus has read it. So has your neighbour, and the official behind the desk, and the person who votes against you. Each of them now knows that they have been underestimating you. Each of them knows that you know the same about them. A false belief that survives because everyone holds it in private cannot easily survive being corrected for everyone, in the open, at the same moment. It may be the only kind of problem that a piece of writing could solve just by being read.

Of course, not everyone will read it. That matters less than it seems. The wallets were coming back long before anyone counted them. The goodwill is already in the room, behind the calm faces, waiting for the same thing you are waiting for.

So go back to the classroom. The teacher asks whether there are any questions. This time a hand goes up, and a voice says, "I'm sorry, I got lost about ten minutes ago. Could you go through it again?"

You know what happens next, because you have seen that too. Shoulders drop. Somebody laughs with relief. Around the room, other hands start to rise.

Nothing in the room has changed except what the people in it know about each other. It took one hand. It can be yours.
